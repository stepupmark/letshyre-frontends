If you read the headlines, AI is about to run hiring end to end. Agents that source, screen,
interview, rank and send the offer while the recruiter sleeps.

Ask the people who do the hiring, and you get a different picture. They're using AI more every
year, and they're almost unanimous about where it should stop.

## More AI, and a very clear line

In [Willo's 2026 hiring trends report](https://www.onrec.com/news/news-archive/hiring-trends-report-2026-study-finds-ai-pushing-employers-away-from-traditional),
65% of hiring teams said they'd increased their use of AI tools, mostly to deal with volume, to
summarise applications and for early screening. In the same survey, not a single respondent thought
automation should handle every stage of hiring.

The survey is small, so treat the exact percentages lightly. The shape of the answer is the
interesting part. Teams are pulling AI in at the top of the funnel and holding it back at the
bottom. The more a step involves judging a person, the less they want a machine doing it alone.

## Where AI is earning its place

Look at what hiring teams say they use it for, and a pattern appears. AI is good at the work that's
mostly reading.

  width={720}

  caption="Our reading of where hiring teams put AI and people today, based on the Willo survey. Shading shows how much of the work each one does."
/>

- **Sourcing and screening.** Reading three hundred applications for the five that meet the brief is
  exactly the kind of tireless, rule-following work software is good at.
- **Summarising.** Turning a long interview or a dense resume into a page someone can read in two
  minutes saves real hours, and a person still reads the page.
- **Running a consistent first interview.** Asking every candidate the same questions in the same
  way is something people are bad at, and software isn't.

What these have in common is that a person still looks at the output before anything happens to a
candidate.

## Why the line holds at the decision

It would be easy to call this caution, or fear of the new. There are better reasons.

**Candidates don't trust it.** Gartner surveyed 3,290 job candidates and found about half of them already
assume AI is reading their applications. Very few believe it will be fair to them.

<Stat
  value="26%"
  label="of job candidates trust AI to evaluate them fairly, while 52% believe AI is already screening their application"
  source="https://www.personneltoday.com/hr/by-2028-one-in-four-candidate-profiles-will-be-fake"
  cite="Gartner survey of 3,290 job candidates, reported by Personnel Today, 2025"
/>

A hiring process that the best candidates don't trust loses some of them before the first call.

**The law is moving the same way.** The EU's AI Act puts AI used to screen, rank or evaluate job
candidates in its high-risk category, which comes with duties around human oversight, record-keeping
and transparency. India's data protection law adds its own duties around consent and how candidate
data is handled. Whatever the exact timelines, the direction is consistent: a person has to stay
accountable for the decision.

**The decision is the part that needs context.** Whether this person fits this team, at this pay,
for this stage of the company, depends on things that never appear in an application. A model can
rank candidates against a job description. It can't know that the team lead is leaving in March.

<Callout tone="warning">
  "Human in the loop" only counts if the human actually looks. A recruiter who
  approves a ranked list without opening it hasn't made a decision; they've
  signed one.
</Callout>

## What good AI-assisted hiring looks like

<Steps>
  <Step title="Use AI where the work is reading">
    Screening for hard requirements, summarising long documents and interviews,
    scheduling. Let it take the volume.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Make every AI judgement inspectable">
    If software scores a candidate, a person should be able to see why: which
    answer, which requirement, which part of the interview. A score with no
    evidence behind it is just an opinion with a decimal point.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Keep the evidence, not just the score">
    Summaries lose detail. Keep the source, whether that's the resume, the
    answers or a recording, so anyone can check the summary against it.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Tell candidates what's automated">
    Say which steps use AI and that a person makes the final call. It's
    increasingly a legal expectation, and it's the cheapest way to close the
    trust gap.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Put a name on every decision">
    Each hire or rejection should have a person who made it and a sentence on
    why. If nobody can explain a rejection, it shouldn't have happened.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Where this is heading

AI in hiring is going to keep growing, and it should. Most of hiring is repetitive reading, and
nobody became a recruiter to read the three hundredth cover letter of the week. AI will get used.
What's worth asking is whether the people using it can still see what it saw.
