Say you're Rohan, a QA lead at an IT services company in Hyderabad on ₹11 lakh. Last April your
rating said "exceeds expectations". Two weeks later the letter said 6%. You'd prepared for the
appraisal meeting for a week, with a slide of everything you'd shipped. It didn't move the number,
because the number had been fixed before the meeting.

That's how most April cycles work. The budget is set early in the year, ratings are compared in a
calibration room you're not in, and the appraisal meeting tells you the result. If you want a
bigger hike in 2027, the conversation that matters happens in December and January.

## The number is decided before the meeting

The calendar differs by company, but for a cycle that pays out in April, the order is usually the
same. Each step is a chance to change the outcome, and the chances get smaller as it goes.

<Steps>
  <Step title="December: ask how the cycle works">
    Ask your manager when budgets are set, when ratings are calibrated, and what
    a top rating usually means in percentage terms. Most managers will tell you
    if you ask plainly.
  </Step>
  <Step title="January: hand over your case, in writing">
    A short note your manager can forward to the calibration meeting. They argue
    for several people at once. Make yours the easiest case to make.
  </Step>
  <Step title="February and March: check in once">
    Ask whether anything else would help. Don't ask for the number yet.
  </Step>
  <Step title="April: respond to the letter">
    If it's lower than you hoped, ask what would change it, and get a date to
    review it again.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Know what a normal year looks like

Averages won't set your hike, but they tell you whether yours is low. Across Indian employers,
salaries rose 9% in 2026, and the same survey expects 9.1% in 2027. The spread between sectors is
wide.

So Rohan's 6% wasn't unusual for IT services. That's useful to know. It means a good rating alone
won't get him much more. He needs a reason his case sits outside the average.

The other thing to know is where you sit in your salary band. Many companies use a grid: your
rating on one side, how far you are into the band on the other. Two people with the same rating get
different hikes if one is paid near the bottom of the band. Ask your manager or HR for your band. If
you're below the middle of it, that's the strongest argument you have.

## Build the case on the job you do now

Calibration meetings reward evidence that's easy to repeat. Your manager has a few minutes to argue
for you, so give them sentences they can say out loud.

<Compare
  avoid="I've worked really hard this year, and my rent went up 20%."
  prefer="Since July I've led test automation for two accounts, not one, and cut the regression cycle from four days to one. That's the scope of a senior lead, and I'm paid at the bottom of the lead band."
/>

The first version is about you. The second is about the job, and it gives the company a reason to
pay more that it can defend to anyone who asks. Personal costs are real, but they can't be argued in
calibration.

A competing offer is the other strong argument, if you're willing to take it. Don't invent one.
Managers can usually tell, and a bluff that gets called leaves you worse off than before. If you
have a real one, compare it on [fixed pay, not CTC](/en/blogs/negotiate-offer-india) before you
bring it up, and read [what a counter-offer fixes](/en/blogs/counter-offer-stay-or-go) in case your
employer matches it.

## Turn the percentage into rupees

Percentages make small gaps sound small. Put in your current <Term id="ctc">CTC</Term> and two outcomes, the hike you
expect and the one you're asking for.

<Tool name="salary-hike" />

For Rohan, 6% against 12% is about ₹4,300 a month, or ₹51,000 a year. On ₹11 lakh he's under the
₹12 lakh rebate, so almost every rupee of it reaches his account. It also carries forward. Every
future hike is a percentage of the new number, so a gap this year stays in his salary for as long as
he's there.

## When the answer is no

Sometimes the budget is the budget. A no on the percentage still leaves room for other things, and
some of them are worth more in a year.

- **A review date in writing.** "Let's revisit in October" is only real with the date and the
  criteria in an email.
- **A title or level change.** It decides your next hike and your next job. We go through [making
  the case for a promotion](/en/blogs/promotion-case) separately.
- **A bigger role.** New scope this year is the evidence for next year's calibration.
- **A one-time bonus.** It doesn't carry forward, but it can close a gap the budget can't.

If the answers stay no for two cycles, the market is telling you something your employer isn't. A
salary check outside the company costs you nothing.

## Start in December

Rohan's best move is a 20-minute conversation before the holidays. He asks how the cycle works and
where he sits in the band, and he sends his case in January, before calibration. When the letter
arrives in April, he'll already know whether it's right.
