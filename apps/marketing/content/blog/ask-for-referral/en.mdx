Say you're Nikhil, a support engineer in Kochi who wants to move into a product company. You find
the right role at a fintech in Bengaluru, where you know nobody. On LinkedIn there's one link: a
senior from your college, four batches above you, who has worked there for two years. You've never
spoken.

You're not asking a stranger for a favour as much as you think. Many companies pay employees a
bonus for referrals that get hired, and a good referral makes the referrer look good too. What makes
them say yes is how little work you leave them: the job link, why you fit in two lines, and a note
they can forward without rewriting.

## Why a referral is worth asking for

An application through the careers page joins a pile that's often screened by keyword or by a
recruiter with a few seconds per CV. A referral arrives with a name attached, and someone reads it.
It won't get you a job you can't do. It gets the CV of someone who can do it looked at.

## Pick the right person

The best referrer knows the team, not the company's org chart.

- **Someone in the same function**, ideally the same team. An engineer referring an engineer
  carries more weight than a director in another department.
- **A real link**: the same college, a former employer, a community you're both in. It gives your
  first line a reason to exist.
- **Someone still there.** Check their profile shows the company as current.

One good person beats ten. Messaging everyone at the company with the same request is the quickest
way to be ignored by all of them.

## The first message: short, specific, easy to say no to

Send it on LinkedIn or by email, and make the whole ask visible without scrolling.

<Compare
  avoid="Hi sir, I am looking for a job change. Please refer me for any suitable opening in your company. Resume attached."
  prefer="Hi Anand, I'm Nikhil, from CUSAT's 2021 batch. I'm applying for the Support Engineer II role at Ledgerline (job ID 4821). I've spent three years on API support for payment integrations, which matches most of the listing. Would you be comfortable referring me? Completely fine if not."
/>

The first message asks them to find you a job. The second asks for one specific thing, shows why
it makes sense, and gives them a polite way out. People say yes more easily when no is easy too.

## Make it forwardable

If they agree, send everything they need in one message, so they can paste it into the referral
form without asking you again.

<Steps>
  <Step title="The job link and ID">
    Not "the support role". Referral forms ask for the exact job.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Three lines about you">
    What you do now, the one result closest to this role, and why this company.
    Written so they can paste it in as their own note.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Your CV as a PDF">
    Named with your name and the role, tailored to this job, one or two pages.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Then leave it with them. One polite follow-up after a week is fine if you haven't heard. After that,
apply directly and stop asking.

## Close the loop, whatever happens

Thank them the day they refer you, and tell them the result, good or bad. Most people who refer
never hear what happened, and the few who do remember the person who told them. If you get the
job, thank them again in person. If you don't, you've still made a contact at a company you wanted
to join.

## Nikhil's message

Nikhil sends his five-line message on a Tuesday evening. Anand replies on Thursday, asks for the
CV, and refers him the same day. The recruiter calls the next week. Not every message gets a
reply, so Nikhil sends one more the following week, to a former colleague at a different company,
the same way.
