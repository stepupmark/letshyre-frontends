Say you're Rohan, a QA lead in Chennai. You've accepted an offer and resigned, and you join in
three weeks. Then an email arrives from the verification firm: "Discrepancy: employment dates at
your second employer don't match records." Your CV says March 2023. Their HR says 14 February.

You didn't lie. February was your last working day, and March was when the <Term id="relieving-letter">relieving letter</Term> came.
But a report that says "discrepancy" reads the same whether you lied or not, until you explain it.
Most mismatches are innocent and take a day to clear, if you find them before the verification firm
does.

## What gets checked

Your new employer will ask you to sign a consent form listing the checks. The usual ones are:

- **Employment.** Each employer's name, your dates and title, sometimes your salary.
- **Education.** Your degree, with the university or board that issued it.
- **Address.** Often with a visit or a geotagged photo.
- **Criminal records and identity**, through court and police databases.
- **References**, usually your last manager.
- **PF history.** Your <Term id="uan">UAN</Term> record shows every employer that paid provident
  fund contributions for you, and for which months.

The last one surprises people most. A job you left off your CV after four months is in the PF
record, and an unexplained one looks like something you hid.

## The mismatches that catch honest people

Most flags come from a handful of ordinary situations.

- **Dates.** Offer date, joining date, last working day and relieving date are all different. Use
  the dates on your relieving letter, and give the last working day as your end date.
- **Titles.** "Senior Engineer" on your email signature may be "Grade B2, Engineer II" in the HR
  system. Write your title as it appears on your relieving or experience letter.
- **Payroll companies.** If you worked at a client through a staffing firm, the staffing firm was
  your employer. Name both: "Employed by Brightline Staffing, deployed at Acme Bank."
- **Renamed or acquired companies.** Use the name on your documents and add the current one.
- **Companies that closed.** Nobody answers the phone, and the report says "unable to verify".
- **Salary.** You quoted <Term id="ctc">CTC</Term>, and they compared it with
  <Term id="fixed-pay">fixed pay</Term>.

## Check your own record first

Before you fill in the verification form, compare what you wrote with what the records will say.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Open your PF service history">
    Log in to the EPFO member portal and look at the service history for your
    UAN. Every employer and every month of contributions is there. Match it to
    your CV, line by line.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Gather your documents">
    Offer and relieving letters, experience letters, your last payslip at each
    job, Form 16, and bank statements showing salary credits. One folder, named
    by employer.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Write the form from the documents">
    Copy dates and titles from your letters, not from your CV or memory. Where
    they differ, the letter wins.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Explain anything unusual in advance">
    A closed company, a payroll firm, a gap or a short job you left off. Two
    sentences to HR, with documents attached, before anyone asks.
  </Step>
</Steps>

For a company that has shut, your tax records do the work. Form 16 and your annual tax statement
show who deducted tax on your salary, and bank statements show the credits. Together they prove
the job existed and when.

## If you're already flagged

Answer the same day, in writing, with documents. Say what the record shows, why it differs, and
attach the proof.

<Compare
  avoid="There must be some mistake. I definitely worked there until March."
  prefer="Your report shows my last day at Northwind as 14 February 2023. That was my last working day. My relieving letter, dated 3 March, is attached, along with my February payslip."
/>

Copy the HR contact at your new employer, so the explanation reaches the person deciding, not only
the verification firm. Most employers clear an innocent mismatch with documents like these. What
they can't accept is no answer.

## Things that turn a mismatch into a lie

Some fixes make it worse. Don't buy an experience letter, ask a friend's company to vouch for you,
or edit a payslip. Employers check the company behind a letter as well as the letter itself, and
we've written about [how they do it](/en/blogs/background-checks-employers). A forged document is
grounds to withdraw an offer, or to dismiss you after you join.

If there's something you'd rather not explain, like a job that ended badly or a gap, explain it
anyway, briefly and first. There's more on that in
[explaining a gap in your CV](/en/blogs/explain-a-career-gap).

<Checklist title="Before you sign the verification form">
  <Check>PF service history matches the employers and dates on your CV</Check>
  <Check>Dates and titles copied from your letters, not from memory</Check>
  <Check>Staffing firm and client both named, where that applies</Check>
  <Check>
    Form 16 and bank statements ready for any company that has closed
  </Check>
  <Check>
    A short note to HR about anything unusual, sent before the check starts
  </Check>
</Checklist>

Rohan sends his relieving letter and February payslip within an hour of the email. The report is
updated to "verified" two days later, and nobody mentions it again. On his next form, he'll use the
dates on his letters from the start.
