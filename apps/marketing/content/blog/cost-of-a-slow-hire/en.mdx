Every hiring manager has a version of this story. The candidate was perfect. Strong interview, good
references, the team loved them. The offer went out on a Thursday. On Friday, they called to say
they'd accepted somewhere else on Tuesday.

Nobody did anything wrong, exactly. The process just took long enough for someone else to finish
first.

## Slow hiring costs more than the vacancy

Most teams think of a slow hire as an empty seat. That's the visible cost: work that doesn't get done,
deadlines that slip, and the rest of the team covering for weeks. It's real, but it's not the
expensive part.

The expensive part is who you lose while you wait. The candidates with the most options are the
ones least willing to sit in your queue.

<Stat
  value="39%"
  label="of workers said they lose interest and pursue other roles when a hiring process drags on"
  source="https://press.roberthalf.com/2016-08-11-Are-You-Taking-Too-Long-To-Hire"
  cite="Robert Half survey of 1,000+ US office workers, 2016"
/>

The same Robert Half survey asked what counts as "too long", and 39% said seven to fourteen days
between the first interview and the offer. That's a short window, and most processes blow through it
without anyone noticing.

Offers are getting harder to close, too. Gartner found that only [51% of candidates accepted a job
offer](https://www.personneltoday.com/hr/by-2028-one-in-four-candidate-profiles-will-be-fake) in
their most recent search, down from 74% two years earlier. When half your offers can fail, every
extra week between interview and offer is a week for something else to win.

## Where the days actually go

For non-executive roles, the median time from opening a role to filling it was
[39 days](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/research/2025-recruiting-benchmarking) in SHRM's
benchmarking data, drawn from more than 4,600 organisations. Thirty-nine days sounds like a lot of
work. It usually isn't.

Look at a real timeline and most of it is waiting: for a budget sign-off, for an interviewer's
calendar, for written feedback, for someone to approve the offer. None of those gaps feel slow to
the people inside the company, because each one is someone being busy with something else. To the
candidate, they're silence.

## Why candidates actually walk away

It's tempting to assume candidates leave for more money. Some do. But when Robert Half asked more than
2,500 workers what would make them withdraw from a hiring process, the answers were mostly about the
process itself.

<Figure
  src={leave}
  srcDark={leaveDark}
  alt="Three bars. 52% would withdraw over poor communication and follow-up, 40% over more than three rounds of interviews, and 39% over a lengthy hiring process"
  width={720}
  height={280}
  caption="All three are about how the company runs its process, not about the job."
/>

More than half pointed to poor communication. That's the cheapest problem in hiring to fix. A candidate
who hears "we're waiting on one more interviewer, you'll have an answer by Wednesday" waits. A
candidate who hears nothing for eight days starts taking other calls.

<Callout>
  Speed and silence aren't the same thing. A three-week process with an update
  every few days feels faster to a candidate than a two-week process with none.
</Callout>

## Faster without being careless

The goal isn't to rush the decision. It's to stop losing days that nobody is using.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Get approval before you open the role">
    Budget, level and salary range agreed up front. The offer stage is the worst
    place to discover the number needs another sign-off.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Book interview slots before you shortlist">
    Hold time in the interviewers' calendars for the week the shortlist lands.
    Scheduling is where many of the grey gaps come from.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Cap the rounds at three">
    If the fourth round would decide anything, one of the first three was asking
    the wrong questions. Fix those instead of adding more.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Feedback within a day">
    Written scores the same day as the interview, before anyone talks to anyone
    else. Memory fades, and late feedback is one of the longest waits in any
    process.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Tell candidates the timeline, then keep to it">
    Say when they'll hear next, and send the update even when the update is "no
    decision yet".
  </Step>
</Steps>
