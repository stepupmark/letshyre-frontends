Say you're Karthik, a backend engineer at a product company in Bengaluru on ₹14 lakh. On Monday you
resign with a ₹19.5 lakh offer from a fintech in your pocket. On Wednesday your manager books a
meeting room, closes the door and says the company will match it. There might be a lead role next
cycle too.

It feels like winning. You get the money without the risk of a new team. But a <Term
id="counter-offer">counter-offer</Term> answers one question, the pay, and you started looking for
more than one reason. Decide on those reasons, and stay only if the counter fixes them in writing.

## Write down why you started looking

Do this before the next meeting, while you still remember. People rarely move for money alone. When
Karthik is honest, his list has three lines:

- He's paid less than the people he's been training.
- He's been "next in line" for a lead role for two cycles.
- His team was told to come to the office four days a week from January.

The counter fixes the first line. The second is a "might", and the third isn't mentioned. If he
stays, two of his three reasons are still there in February.

<Callout tone="tip">
  Score each reason: fixed by the counter, promised, or not addressed. Only the
  first kind is real until it's in an email or a letter.
</Callout>

## Read the counter like a new offer

A counter is easy to accept because it arrives in a hurry and in a friendly voice. Read it as
carefully as you read the offer that caused it. These are the questions that change the answer most
often.

- **Is it fixed pay, or something else?** A one-time retention bonus looks like a raise and isn't
  one. It often comes with a [clause that makes you pay it
  back](/en/blogs/joining-bonus-clawback) if you leave within a year.
- **Is it your April hike, early?** If the company would have given you 8 or 9% at appraisal anyway,
  ask whether you'll still get that hike, or whether the counter replaces it.
- **Does it come with the title?** "Lead role next cycle" with no date and no criteria is a hope. A
  new title in the revision letter is a fact.
- **Who approved it?** A counter your manager got from HR in two days was available all along. That
  tells you something about how pay decisions are made there.

The second question hides the biggest gap. Average hikes at Indian technology product companies
were 9.5% in 2026, [according to Aon](https://www.aon.com/apac/in-the-press/asia-newsroom/2026/aon-study-finds-salaries-in-india-projected-to-fise-9-1-in-2027-employers-balance-growth-and-cost-pressures).
If Karthik's match quietly replaces the next appraisal, part of the jump is money he'd have been
paid anyway.

Put both versions into the calculator. The first is his ₹14 lakh against the matched ₹19.5 lakh. The
second is ₹15.26 lakh, his salary after a 9% appraisal, against the same match.

## What changes once you've resigned

You'll see the claim that 80% of people who accept a counter-offer leave within six months. We
couldn't find the study it comes from, so don't decide on that number. What's easier to check is
what has changed around you since Monday.

Your manager now knows you'll leave for the right offer. That isn't always bad, and some managers
respect it. But it can change who gets the next stretch project and who's on the list when budgets
tighten. Ask yourself one plain question: if the company had valued you at ₹19.5 lakh, why did it
take a resignation to say so?

There's a second relationship too. The fintech's recruiter may have turned down other candidates
for you. Taking their offer and then a counter in your last week costs more goodwill than saying no
on day two.

## Before you say yes

<Checklist title="Before you accept a counter-offer">
  <Check>
    The reasons I started looking are written down, and the counter fixes each
    one.
  </Check>
  <Check>
    The new pay is fixed pay, in a revision letter, with an effective date.
  </Check>
  <Check>I know whether the counter replaces my next appraisal hike.</Check>
  <Check>Any title or role change is in writing, with a date.</Check>
  <Check>There's no clause making me repay part of it if I leave.</Check>
  <Check>I've told the other company, or will tell them within a day.</Check>
</Checklist>

## How to say no to the counter

If the counter doesn't fix your reasons, say no early and say it kindly. You'll work with these
people for another 60 or 90 days, and you may want a reference later.

<Compare
  avoid="Honestly, if you'd paid me this a year ago I wouldn't be leaving."
  prefer="Thank you, I know that took effort to put together. I've thought about it carefully, and I'm going to take the other role. I'll make sure the handover is in good shape."
/>

Then make the <Term id="notice-period">notice period</Term> count. Agree a handover plan in writing,
ask HR when your <Term id="relieving-letter">relieving letter</Term> will be ready, and check
whether a <Term id="notice-buyout">buyout</Term> could shorten your notice. We've written about [planning a job change around
your notice](/en/blogs/notice-period-job-hunt).

## If you decide to stay

Staying is a fair choice when the counter fixes what was wrong. Do three things that week. Get every
promise into a revision letter. Write to the other company the same day, thank them and say you're
staying. Then put a date in your calendar, six months out, to check whether the reasons on your list
are still fixed.

That's what Karthik should do on Thursday morning: open the note with his three reasons and put the
counter next to each one. If only the pay line is fixed, he already knows the answer, and the
fintech's recruiter should hear it before lunch.
