There's a particular silence that follows "So, what were you doing between March and November?" Most
people fill it with an apology. They explain too much, too fast, and by the end of it the
interviewer is thinking about the gap more than they were before the question.

It doesn't have to go that way. A career gap is a fact about your life, like where you studied or
which city you live in. You don't need to defend it. You need to explain it once, clearly, and move
on.

## You're in the majority

The first thing to know is how ordinary this is. When LinkedIn surveyed more than 23,000 workers and
4,000 hiring managers in 2022, most people had stepped away from work at some point.

<Stat

The same survey found two things worth holding on to. About one in five hiring managers said they
would reject a candidate who'd taken a break, so the worry isn't imaginary. But 51% said they would
likely call a candidate back if they knew why the break happened.

That second number is the useful one. The gap itself rarely loses people the job. The mystery does.

## Put it on the resume, briefly

Leaving a gap unexplained invites the reader to guess, and people guess the worst. A single line in
your work history answers the question before anyone has to ask it.

Keep it to one line and a reason. "Career break, caring for a family member." "Career break,
relocation and a cloud certification." "Career break after a company-wide layoff." You don't owe
anyone a diagnosis, a breakup story or the details of a severance package.

<Callout tone="tip">
  If you did something during the break that's relevant to the job, such as a
  course or a freelance project, add it under the break as a bullet. If you
  didn't, that's fine too. Rest is a reason.
</Callout>

## The thirty-second answer

When the question comes in the interview, and it often will, the best answer has three parts and
fits in about thirty seconds.

<Steps>
  <Step title="What happened">
    One sentence, plain and specific. "I was laid off in a restructuring" is
    better than "I decided to explore new opportunities", which nobody believes.
  </Step>
  <Step title="What changed">
    Why the reason no longer gets in the way. The situation resolved, the course
    finished, the move is done.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Why now, and why this role">
    End on what you want to do next. This hands the conversation back to the
    job, which is where you want it.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Answers for the common reasons

Every gap is different, but most fall into a few shapes. Here's how each tends to land best.

- **Layoff.** Say it directly and early. Layoffs have touched so many companies that "my role was cut
  in a restructuring" carries much less weight than it used to. Don't criticise the old employer.
- **Caring for family.** Say who and that it's resolved or supported now. You don't need to say more.
- **Health.** "I took time off for a health issue, and I'm fully back now" is enough. You're not
  obliged to share details, and a good interviewer won't ask.
- **Study or a career change.** This is the easiest one. Lead with what you learned and how it
  connects to the role.
- **A long job search.** Be honest about it and talk about what you did with the time. Many good
  people have had a slow search in a slow market.
- **Burnout or a deliberate break.** Frame it around what you decided, not what went wrong. "I'd
  worked without a real break for six years, and I took four months to reset before choosing what
  to do next."

## Things that make a gap look bigger than it is

- **Apologising.** Saying sorry tells the interviewer you think it's a problem. They'll take your word
  for it.
- **Long explanations.** The more you say, the more questions it raises. Thirty seconds, then stop.
- **Fudging dates.** Stretching a job's end date to cover the gap is the one thing that turns a
  non-issue into a real one. Background checks catch it, and then the question is about honesty.
- **Bringing it up first in every answer.** Mention it once, when it's relevant. Then talk about your
  work.
