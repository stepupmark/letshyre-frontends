Say you're Aditya, a test engineer in Chennai with three years' experience. A large IT services
company offers you ₹9.5 lakh at its B2 level. You ask for 11. The recruiter is polite and firm:
"Bands are fixed here. Everyone at B2 gets the same offer."

That's often true, and arguing with it wastes the call. But a fixed band fixes one number. The level
you're placed at, the date you join, the cash around the salary and your next review are usually
decided by someone else. Over three years, they often matter more than a lakh this year.

## Ask about the level, not the number

In companies with strict bands, the level is the salary. Moving from B2 to B3 can be worth more than
any negotiation inside B2, and the level is decided by mapping your experience to a grid. That
mapping is a judgment, and judgments can be reviewed.

Bring evidence that matches the next level's description, not your current title. If the company
publishes its levels on its careers site or in the offer pack, use their words.

If the answer is no, ask what B3 needs and when you'd next be considered. Get that in writing too.

## Check where you sit inside the band

"Fixed" sometimes means a range, and offers start at its bottom. Ask a direct question: is this the
minimum, the midpoint or the maximum of B2? If it's the minimum and your experience is at the top
of the level's range, that's a reason to move within the band, even where the band itself can't
move.

## Pick a joining date that gets you into the next cycle

Many companies with an April appraisal only include people who joined before a cut-off, often in
the autumn. Join a week after it and your first hike can be 18 months away. Ask HR for the cut-off
before you agree a date. It costs nothing to ask and can be worth a year's raise.

The same goes for your <Term id="notice-period">notice period</Term>. If your current employer will
release you early, or the new one will cover a buyout, you may make a cut-off you'd otherwise miss.

## Ask for the money that sits outside the band

Bands usually cover fixed pay. Other budgets often sit with a different team and have their own
rules.

- **A <Term id="joining-bonus">joining bonus</Term>**, especially to make up a bonus you'd forfeit by leaving now. Read [the
  repayment clause](/en/blogs/joining-bonus-clawback) before you count it.
- **Notice buyout reimbursement**, if you're paying to leave early.
- **Relocation**, if you're moving cities: travel, a few weeks of housing and shifting costs.
- **Certification or course fees**, which some teams pay from a training budget.

## Ask for terms that cost nothing

Some of the most useful asks have no rupee value on the day you sign.

<Steps>
  <Step title="A first review at six months">
    With the criteria agreed now. It brings your first raise forward if you
    deliver.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The team or account you join">
    Ask which project you'd be placed on. Some accounts lead to promotions
    faster than others, and people inside know which.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Location and office days">
    A posting near home, or one more remote day a week, has a real cash value
    once you count the commute.
  </Step>
  <Step title="A shorter notice period in your new contract">
    It's easier to agree before you join, and it matters the next time you move.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Ask for two of these, not all four. A list reads as a wish list, and a single clear ask reads as a
priority.

## What Aditya should send

A short reply the same day: thanks for the offer, a request to review the level with two lines of
evidence, and a question about the appraisal cut-off. If B3 comes back, the band was never the
problem. If it doesn't, he still has a date that puts him in April's cycle, and a six-month review
in writing.
