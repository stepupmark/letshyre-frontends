Say you run engineering at a 300-person SaaS company in Bengaluru. Last year you hired 14
graduates. This year the plan from your leads has three. Their reasoning is sound: coding assistants
now clear the small tickets that used to go to juniors, and seniors with an assistant ship faster
than seniors with a junior.

The tickets were never only work. They were how a 22-year-old learned your codebase, your customers
and your standards, and how you got mid-level engineers you didn't have to buy. Stop now, and in
2029 you'll be hiring every mid-level engineer at market rate from the companies that didn't stop.
The answer for most teams is fewer freshers, hired for different reasons, with a job built for
them.

## The market has already split

Not everyone is cutting. Global capability centres raised their share of fresher hiring from 8% to
11% over the same year, the biggest gain of any employer type. In TeamLease EdTech's survey of 1,097
employers, three in four
[plan to hire freshers](https://www.peoplematters.in/news/workforce-planning/fresher-hiring-intent-rises-to-75percent-but-it-demand-loses-ground-report-51352)
in the second half of 2026, though IT's intent fell from 81% to 76%.

The companies still hiring are asking for something different: applied problem-solving, AI fluency
and project work they can see. They've changed what the junior role is, rather than deleting it.

## What the junior tickets were for

Think about what a first-year engineer did on your team two years ago. Some of it was cheap
capacity: the CSS fix, the extra endpoint, the migration nobody wanted. Most of the value was
elsewhere.

- They learned the codebase by changing it in small, safe places.
- They built judgment by having their work reviewed, line by line, by someone better.
- They asked the questions seniors had stopped asking, and some of those found real problems.
- In two or three years, they became the mid-level engineers who knew your systems.

An assistant does the first part well. It does none of the others, and the last one is the expensive
one to replace.

## The work moved, it didn't vanish

AI-written code still has to be read, tested, debugged and run in production. Someone has to check
that the generated migration won't lock a table at peak hours. Someone has to work out why the
feature passes every test and still fails for one customer.

That checking work is where juniors fit now. It teaches the same things the tickets did, and the
team needs more of it than before, because the volume of code has gone up.

## Rewrite the junior role

<Steps>
  <Step title="Give each fresher something small to own">
    A service, an internal tool or one integration, end to end, with an
    assistant and a named reviewer. Ownership teaches faster than a queue of
    tickets.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Make review the main job for the first three months">
    Freshers review AI-generated changes before a senior does, and write down
    what they found. The senior reviews the review.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Rotate them through support and on-call">
    Two weeks reading customer tickets and incidents teaches more about your
    product than any onboarding deck.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Budget the seniors' time">
    Four hours a week for six months is about 100 hours per fresher. Put it in
    the plan, or it won't happen.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Hire for checking, not producing

If the job is reviewing and owning code an assistant helped write, the interview should test that.
A timed problem from scratch tells you who practised timed problems.

<Compare
  avoid="Reverse a linked list in 20 minutes, without help."
  prefer="Here's a pull request an assistant wrote. It passes the tests. Tell us what you'd check before merging it, and what you'd ask the author."
/>

Plant two problems in the pull request, one obvious and one that needs thought about real data. The
graduates worth hiring find the first, ask good questions about the second, and say what they're
unsure of. We've written about [hiring for skills over degrees](/en/blogs/skills-over-degrees),
which applies doubly here.

## Hire fewer, and keep them

The leads aren't wrong that 14 is too many for the new shape of the work. Three is too few to give
you a mid-level bench in 2029, especially as some will leave.

Say you settle on six, each with an owner, a reviewer and a budgeted 100 hours. Write down the four
things each one should own by month six, and check at the end. That's a smaller graduate programme,
and a much more deliberate one, and it's still yours in three years.
