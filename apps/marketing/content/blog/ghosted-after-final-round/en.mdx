Say you're Divya, a data engineer in Pune. Your final round was on a Thursday: a system design
session and a long talk with the hiring manager, who walked you to the lift and said she hoped to
work with you. The recruiter said you'd hear by Tuesday. It's now the Friday after, and your inbox
has nothing.

Silence after a final round is usually a delay, not a decision. Approvals sit in someone's queue,
budgets get rechecked, or they're waiting on another candidate. But you can't plan your life around
"usually". Follow up twice, on a schedule, keep your search running, and set a deadline of your own.

## Why it goes quiet

Companies rarely go silent because they've rejected you. A rejection is one email, and most
recruiters would rather send it than field your follow-ups. Silence more often means one of these.

- **Approvals.** The offer needs sign-off from finance or a leader who is travelling.
- **A second candidate.** You may be the backup while they wait for someone else's answer.
- **A budget or headcount check**, sometimes after a reorganisation nobody has announced.
- **A recruiter with 40 open roles**, who hasn't had a reply to give you.

None of these is about you, and two of them still end in an offer. That's why the first follow-up
should be easy to answer, not a complaint.

## The first follow-up: one working day after their date

If they gave you a date, write the next working day after it passes. If they didn't, wait five
working days. Send it to the recruiter, by email, and keep it to three sentences.

<Compare
  avoid="Hi, I haven't heard anything since my interview. Can you please update me on the status? I've been waiting for over a week."
  prefer="Hi Meghna, thank you again for organising Thursday's round. I enjoyed the design discussion with Priya. Is there an update on the timeline? I'm still very interested in the role."
/>

The second version reminds them who you are, names the person you met, and asks one question they
can answer in a line. It also doesn't sound like you're keeping score.

## The second follow-up: a week later, with something new

If the first gets nothing, wait a week. This time, add a reason to reply: a new date in your own
process, or one thing you've thought about since the interview. If you have the hiring manager's
email or are connected on LinkedIn, you can copy them, once.

A real deadline is the strongest reason. If another company has made you an offer, say so plainly,
with the date you need to answer by. Don't invent one. Recruiters can usually tell, and if it gets
called, you've lost the one company you wanted. We've written up
[how to word that message](/en/blogs/negotiate-offer-messages).

## Keep your search running

The mistake that costs the most isn't the follow-up. It's what people stop doing while they wait.
Don't resign, decline other interviews or turn down an offer for a role you haven't been offered.
Keep applying until you have a signed letter.

## Set your own deadline, then close it politely

After two follow-ups and three weeks, treat it as a no, whatever arrives later. Send one closing
message. It costs nothing, and it's the one most likely to get a reply.

> Hi Meghna, I haven't heard back, so I'll assume the role has moved on without me. I enjoyed
> meeting the team and would be glad to hear about future roles. Thank you for your help.

A surprising number of these get an answer within a day, sometimes an offer, sometimes the reason.
Either way, you can stop checking your inbox.

## What Divya sends

On Wednesday, the day after their date, Divya sends three sentences to the recruiter. The next
Wednesday she sends the second, mentioning an offer she's expecting from another company in ten
days. The recruiter replies that afternoon: the offer was waiting on one approval, and it arrives on
Friday. If it hadn't, she had a closing message drafted and two other interviews booked.
