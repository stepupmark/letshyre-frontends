Most people get promoted to lead for being the best person on the team at the work. Then, in their
first month, they discover the job is no longer the work.

Your calendar fills with one-to-ones. Your best hours go to unblocking other people. At the end of a
busy week, you can't point to a single thing you made. It's easy to come out of that first year
feeling like you've got worse at your job.

You haven't. The job has changed, and nobody told you how.

## Why it matters who leads

It's tempting to treat a lead role as a reward: a better title for the person who's been there
longest or ships the most. The research says it's much more than that.

<Stat
  value="70%"
  label="or more of the variance in employee engagement scores between teams comes down to the manager"
  source="https://www.gallup.com/workplace/236570/managers-account-variance-team-engagement.aspx"

/>

The person leading a team shapes how much of the team's ability shows up in its work. That's why
interviewers for lead roles ask such different questions, and why "I'm the strongest engineer here"
is rarely enough to get the job.

## What actually changes

The shift isn't from doing work to doing no work. It's from your own output to the team's.

As a lead, you measure the team's output, make sure the hard problem gets solved, give feedback every week and own the priorities. Arrows connect each pair"

The hardest of these, for most people, is the second row. You've been rewarded for years for taking
the hardest problem and solving it. Now, if you take it, the person who needed to learn from it
doesn't, and the six things only you can do sit waiting while you're heads-down.

<Compare

Both stories end with a shipped release. Only the second one shows someone leading.

## Show you're ready before you get the title

Hiring managers for lead roles, whether internal or external, look for one thing above all: evidence
that you've already done parts of the job. The good news is that almost all of it can be done
without the title.

  src={ladder}
  srcDark={ladderDark}
  alt="Four steps rising from left to right, each a way to lead without the title. Mentor one person through a real piece of work. Run a recurring meeting or process for the team. Own a decision that affects more than your own work. Lead a project from plan to delivery, with others doing most of the building"
  width={720}
  height={280}
  caption="Each step is a bit closer to the real job. You don't need all four, but you need stories from at least two."
/>

<Steps>
  <Step title="Mentor someone through real work">
    Not a coffee chat. Help a newer colleague ship something they couldn't have
    shipped alone, and let them take the credit.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Run something the team depends on">
    The weekly planning meeting, the on-call rota, the code review process.
    Whatever it is, make it run better than it did.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Own a decision bigger than your tasks">
    Choose the approach, write down why, and live with the result. Leads are
    judged on their decisions much more than their output.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Lead a project where others do the building">
    This is the closest thing to the real job, and it's the story interviewers
    will ask for first.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## What lead interviews actually test

Interviews for lead roles still check your technical depth. But the questions that decide them are
usually about people and judgement.

- **"Tell me about a time someone on your team was struggling."** They want to know whether you
  noticed, what you did, and whether you'd talk to the person or around them.
- **"How did you handle a disagreement about priorities?"** They're listening for whether you can
  say no and keep the relationship.
- **"What would you stop doing yourself if you got this role?"** A surprisingly honest test. "Nothing"
  is the wrong answer.
- **"Tell me about a decision that turned out wrong."** Leads make more decisions with less
  information. They want to hear that you can own a bad call and fix it.

Every one of these needs a real story. The structure we use for [talking about your
work](/en/blogs/talk-about-your-work) works here too, with one change: your part is usually what
you made possible, not what you built.

<Callout tone="tip">
  Before a lead interview, go through your five best stories and replace every
  "I built" with what you decided, who you helped and what the team shipped.
  The stories that still hold up are your lead stories.
</Callout>

## It's a choice, not a ladder

One last thing. Leading isn't the only way up. Many companies now have senior individual
contributor tracks, staff and principal roles, where the job is deep technical work with influence
across teams. If what you love is the work itself, that path is worth asking about before you take a
lead role because it's the only promotion on offer.

If you do want to lead, start now, in the role you already have. The title tends to follow the
work.
