Say you're hiring a data analyst. You open last year's job description to reuse it, and the first
requirement is "Bachelor's degree in a quantitative field". The second is "Advanced SQL". The third
is "Strong communication skills". None of them tells a candidate what they'd be doing in their
first month, and none of them tells you how you'll pick between the people who apply.

A job description that hires on skills starts somewhere else: with the work. Write down what the
person will own in their first six months, the skills that work takes, and how you'll check each
one. Anything you can't check comes out.

## Removing the degree line is the easy part

Plenty of companies have taken degree requirements out of their job ads. Far fewer changed who
they hired. Researchers at Harvard and the Burning Glass Institute tracked hiring at firms that
dropped the requirement. About 45% had made the change in name only, and the shift showed up
in [fewer than 1 in 700 hires](https://pw.hks.harvard.edu/post/skills-based-hiring-the-long-road-from-pronouncements-to-practice).
We went through that research in
[a separate post](/en/blogs/skills-over-degrees).

The pattern is easy to recognise. The ad loses a line, the screen stays the same, and the same
backgrounds get through. A skills-based job description only works if every skill on it leads to
a step in the process that tests it.

## Start from the first six months

Before you write a single requirement, write the outcomes. Ask the hiring manager what this person
will have done by month six if the hire goes well. Keep it to three or four things, in plain
words.

For the analyst, that might be:

- Rebuilt the weekly sales report so it runs without manual fixes.
- Answered the pricing team's questions about discounting with numbers they trust.
- Explained a finding to the leadership team in a meeting, without a data person in the room.

Those three lines say more about the job than any list of adjectives, and candidates can tell
straight away whether it's work they've done before.

## Turn each outcome into a skill you can check

Now go through the outcomes one by one and ask what someone needs to be able to do to deliver it.
Then ask how you'll see that in the hiring process. If you can't answer the second question, the
requirement doesn't belong in the ad yet.

<Compare
  avoid="Advanced SQL. Strong communication skills. 3 to 5 years of experience in analytics."
  prefer="You'll write SQL against our sales tables in a 45-minute work sample. You'll walk us through one analysis you've presented to people outside your team, and we'll ask what you'd change."
/>

The checks don't need to be elaborate. A short work sample on realistic data shows the SQL.
A <Term id="structured-interview">structured interview</Term>, the same questions for everyone,
scored against the same notes, shows the rest. Structured interviews are among the
[best predictors of later job performance](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000994) that researchers
have measured, which is why they're worth the hour it takes to write the questions.

Put the checks in the job description itself. Candidates prepare better when they know what's
coming, and the ones who can't do the work tend to select themselves out.

## Cut what you won't check

Most job descriptions carry lines nobody tests. "Years of experience" ranges are the usual example.
Three to five years says little about what someone can do, and nobody interviews for the
difference between four years and six. In practice the line is a filter on background, which is
the thing you set out to stop filtering on.

Go through what's left and sort it into two groups:

- **Must have on day one**, and checked in the process.
- **Can learn in the first months**, and said as much in the ad.

The second group matters more than it looks. A requirement listed as essential puts off people who
could learn it in six weeks, and those are often the candidates a skills-based process is meant to
find.

## Write it so the right people recognise themselves

A good job description reads like a description of the job. Use the words your team uses, name the
tools, and say what's hard about the role. "The data is messy and nobody owns the definitions yet"
puts off fewer good candidates than you'd expect, and it saves the ones who'd hate it a round of
interviews.

Keep the company pitch to a paragraph. Candidates read the outcomes and the checks first, because
that's where they find out whether the job is theirs to win.

## Before you post the next role

Take the job description you're about to publish and write the check next to each requirement: the
work sample, the interview question or the reference that will show it. Delete every requirement
that has nothing next to it. For the analyst, that leaves three outcomes, two checks and no degree
line, and when the applications come in, you'll know what you're looking for in each one.
