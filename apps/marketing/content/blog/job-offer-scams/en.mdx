Say you're Pooja, a commerce graduate in Indore, three months into a job search. A WhatsApp message
arrives from "Ananya, HR": part-time work, ₹3,000 to ₹8,000 a day, rating hotels from your phone.
You try it. Twenty minutes later, ₹150 lands in your UPI account.

That ₹150 is the bait. The next day there's a Telegram group, a "merchant task" that needs a ₹5,000
deposit, and a balance on a website that grows faster than seems possible. Withdrawing it needs one
more payment, then another. A real employer never asks you to pay, and doesn't pay you before
you've been hired. Hold any offer to those two rules and most scams fall apart.

Job and task scams are one of the main categories in that figure. They work because they arrive
when people most want good news.

## Money only flows one way

In a real hiring process, money moves from the employer to you, after you've joined. Anything that
reverses that is the scam, whatever it's called.

- **Fees.** Registration, training, a laptop, "document verification", a background check, a
  refundable deposit. Employers pay for their own hiring.
- **Tasks that pay first.** Liking videos, rating hotels or products, with small payouts that turn
  into deposits.
- **Payment "jobs".** Receiving money into your bank account and passing it on for a commission.
  That's laundering, and the account holder is the person the police find. Banks freeze these
  accounts.

## The scam may look like a hiring process

The newer scams copy what real companies do. There's a job post, a video interview, an offer
letter on a logo and a start date. The tell is usually in what they ask for after the offer.

Some ask for Aadhaar, PAN, bank details and a "face verification" video before you've signed
anything, or a video of your face or eyes. In May 2026 the Home Ministry's cybercrime centre
[warned](https://newsable.asianetnews.com/amp/india/mha-warns-job-seekers-of-fake-online-interviews-ai-biometric-scams-articleshow-gy3y9qr)
that fake interviews are collecting exactly that, and that the data can be used to take over
accounts linked to your identity. A real employer collects documents during
onboarding, through its HR system, after you've accepted.

## Check the offer yourself

Don't use the links, numbers or emails the recruiter gives you. Find the company yourself and work
back to the offer.

<Checklist title="Before you reply to a job offer">
  <Check>
    Find the company's careers page through a search, not a link, and look for
    the role
  </Check>
  <Check>
    Check the email domain matches the company's website, not Gmail or a
    lookalike
  </Check>
  <Check>
    Call the company's listed number and ask HR to confirm the offer and the
    recruiter
  </Check>
  <Check>
    Look up the recruiter on LinkedIn and check how long they've worked there
  </Check>
  <Check>
    Read the offer for a salary breakup, reporting manager and address you can
    verify
  </Check>
  <Check>
    Refuse any payment, deposit or app download as a condition of joining
  </Check>
</Checklist>

Pressure is also a signal. "Only three seats left", "the offer expires tonight" and a recruiter who
won't speak on a normal call are there to stop you running exactly these checks.

<Compare
  avoid="Okay, I'll pay the ₹2,500 for the laptop now so I can join on Monday."
  prefer="Our policy is not to pay for anything before joining. Please share the offer from your company email, and I'll confirm it with your HR team on the number on your website."
/>

A real recruiter answers that without fuss. A scammer stops replying or pushes harder, and either
answer tells you what you need.

## If you've already paid

Speed matters more than embarrassment. Money reported within the first hours has the best chance of
being frozen before it moves on.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Call 1930">
    The national cyber fraud helpline. Have the transaction IDs, UPI handles and
    account numbers you paid into ready.
  </Step>
  <Step title="File a complaint on cybercrime.gov.in">
    Add screenshots of the chats, the group names and every payment. Keep the
    complaint number.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Tell your bank">
    Ask them to block further debits and to flag the receiving accounts. Change
    your UPI PIN if you shared anything.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Report the number">
    Use Chakshu on the government's [Sanchar
    Saathi](https://sancharsaathi.gov.in) portal, so the number can be checked
    and blocked.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Then ignore anyone who offers to recover the money for a fee. That's the same group, or another one
that reads the same complaint forums.

## Pooja's next offer

Pooja stops at the ₹5,000 deposit and loses only an evening. Her next offer comes from a company
she applied to herself. She checks the email domain, calls the number on its website, and the HR
team confirms the role. That five-minute call is the habit worth keeping for every offer, including
the real ones.
