Say you're Karthik, a product manager in Pune, six years at the same company. On a Tuesday at
10:30 a meeting called "Quick sync" appears in your calendar. It lasts 14 minutes. By noon your
laptop is locked, and an email offers three months' pay if you sign a separation agreement by
Friday.

The first three days decide most of the money, and they arrive when you're least able to think.
Don't sign yet, collect your documents while HR still answers quickly, and leave your PF alone. The
job search can start next week, with a much better story than you'd tell today.

## Don't sign on the first day

A separation agreement trades money for promises. You usually agree not to make any claim against
the company, and often to confidentiality and a period of not hiring its staff. Read it twice, and
ask for what's missing in writing before you sign.

Ask for a few more days if the deadline is short. Most companies give them, and the request is
normal. Then ask HR to confirm, in one email, what the package includes.

- Notice pay, and whether it's on basic or gross salary
- Severance, and how many weeks per year of service it's based on
- <Term id="gratuity">Gratuity</Term>, if you've done five years
- Payment for unused earned leave
- This year's bonus, in full or pro-rata
- ESOPs: whether unvested options accelerate, and how long you have to exercise
- Health insurance: the date cover ends, and whether it can be extended
- Outplacement help, and a reference or a letter confirming the role was eliminated

The last item is cheap for the company and useful to you. A future employer's background check
reads "role eliminated in a restructuring" very differently from a blank.

## Know which part the law guarantees

The Industrial Relations Code, in force since November 2025, sets a floor for people it counts as
"workers": a month's notice or pay instead, and 15 days' average pay for every completed year of
service. The employer also pays 15 days' wages into a reskilling fund for each person retrenched.

People employed mainly as managers are outside that definition, and so are supervisors earning over
₹18,000 a month. For them, the contract and the company's policy decide. Courts look at what you do,
not your title, so an engineer with nobody reporting to them may well count. If the amounts are
large and the offer looks thin, an hour with an employment lawyer before you sign is worth it.

Karthik manages people, so his package is whatever he can agree. Six years also means he's owed
gratuity, about three and a half months of basic pay, which the law says must be paid within 30 days. He
checks it's listed separately and not folded into the three months they offered.

## Collect your documents before the access goes

Your company email, payroll portal and HR system will go dark, some of them the same day. Ask for
copies of everything you'll need, and don't take company files or code with you.

The breakup matters most. Check it against your last payslip, line by line, and ask about anything
you don't recognise. The <Term id="full-and-final-settlement">full and final settlement</Term> is
due quickly under the new wage rules, so a vague "45 to 60 days" is worth questioning politely.

## Work out your runway, and leave PF alone

Add up your settlement, your savings and your monthly costs, including the EMI and the insurance
you'll now pay yourself. That number tells you how long you can search without accepting the first
offer.

Your provident fund is not part of the runway. Since the EPFO's newer rules, you can withdraw up to
75% at once if you're out of work, and the rest after 12 months. Withdrawing before five years of
service makes it taxable, and at any length of service it's the retirement money you won't rebuild
easily. Use it last, after savings and the settlement.

Health cover is the other early decision. If the group policy ends with your job, ask the insurer
whether you can move to an individual policy with them. Many let you keep credit for the waiting
periods you've already served, which a brand-new policy won't.

## Tell people plainly

Layoffs are common enough now that recruiters don't hold one against you. What they notice is how
you describe it. Say it once, with a fact, and move to your work.

## You're an immediate joiner now

Most candidates in India come with a 60 or 90-day <Term id="notice-period">notice period</Term>.
You can start next week. For a team that needed someone yesterday, that's worth something, so say it
in your first message to every recruiter.

Don't let it lower your number. Base your expectation on the role and the market, not on how long
you've been searching, as in [answering the salary expectations question](/en/blogs/salary-expectations).
Be wary of offers that arrive too easily, too, because [fake recruiters](/en/blogs/job-offer-scams)
look for people who are between jobs.

## Your first 30 days

<Steps>
  <Step title="Days 1 to 3: read, ask, collect">
    Get the package in writing, ask for more time if needed, and collect every
    document. Don't post about it yet.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Days 4 to 7: settle the money">
    Sign once you understand what you're agreeing to. Work out your runway, sort
    health cover, and take a few days off on purpose.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Week 2: tell your network">
    Write your two sentences and send them to 20 people who know your work, one
    at a time. Ask each for one introduction, not for a job.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Weeks 3 and 4: apply where you fit">
    Ten applications you've thought about beat a hundred you haven't. Keep a
    list of where each one stands, and follow up after a week.
  </Step>
</Steps>

On day 30, Karthik has a signed agreement that lists his gratuity separately, a policy that kept
his waiting periods, and four conversations with teams who needed someone fast. He didn't solve it
on Tuesday. He just didn't make the expensive decisions that day.

<Callout tone="warning">
  General guidance, not legal advice. What you're owed depends on your contract,
  your company's policy and whether the labour codes treat you as a worker. Ask
  an employment lawyer if you're unsure.
</Callout>
