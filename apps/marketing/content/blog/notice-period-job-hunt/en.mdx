You've handed in your resignation. Your manager has stopped inviting you to planning meetings. And
somewhere between the farewell lunch and your last day, you're supposed to find your next job.

In a lot of countries, that gap is two weeks. In India, it's often two or three months, and that
changes the whole search. Some employers will love that you're already free to move. Others will
quietly drop you the moment they hear "90 days".

Here's how to make the long notice period work for you instead of against you.

## First, read your own contract

Notice periods in India are set by your contract, not by one national rule. Before you plan anything,
find your appointment letter and any later policy updates, and check three things:

- **How long your notice is**, and whether it changes with seniority or after confirmation.
- **Whether early release is allowed**, and who can approve it.
- **How a buyout is calculated**, if your employer allows one at all: on basic pay or gross pay.

<Callout tone="warning">
  This post is general guidance, not legal advice. If your contract is unclear,
  or your employer is refusing to relieve you, ask HR in writing or speak to an
  employment lawyer.
</Callout>

## Treat the notice period as a project

The mistake most people make is to treat their notice as dead time, then panic in the last three
weeks. Splitting it into three phases keeps both jobs, the one you're leaving and the one you're
looking for, on track.

### The first month, hand over and open doors

Your handover is your reference. The people who'll vouch for you in six months are watching how you
leave. Write down what you know, introduce your replacement to the people they'll need, and close
what you can.

At the same time, start conversations, not applications. Tell trusted ex-colleagues you're looking.
Update your profile. This is the month to find out which companies are hiring for your kind of role,
before you need an answer from any of them.

### The second month, interview in earnest

This is when your notice period becomes an advantage. You can interview without sneaking out of the
office, and your start date is close enough to be real. Most employers hiring experienced people
expect to wait. What they hate is surprises.

### The last month, lock it down

Once you have an offer, the work shifts to making sure the start date holds. Get your relieving
date confirmed by your current employer, agree any buyout in writing, and make sure your new
employer knows the exact date you can join.

## Lead with your date, not your notice

When a recruiter asks about your notice period, the worst answer is the one in your contract. "My
notice is 90 days" makes them count forward from today. "I'm serving my notice now, and I can join
on 14 January" gives them a date they can plan around.

<Compare
  avoid="My notice period is 90 days."
  prefer="I resigned on 16 October, so I'm free to join from 14 January. If you need me sooner, I can ask about early release."
/>

If you're already serving notice, say so early, in your profile and in the first call. Employers
filling senior roles quickly will often move faster for someone who can join in weeks rather than
months. And be honest about the date. A start date that slips after an offer is one of the fastest
ways to lose goodwill with a new employer.

## Understand the buyout before you're asked about it

Some employers let you leave early by paying for the days you don't serve. Some new employers will
offer to cover that cost. The usual calculation is simple, but the details matter.

A few things worth knowing before you agree to one:

- **Whether it's basic or gross pay changes the number a lot.** Check your letter, not a colleague's.
- **Your current employer doesn't have to accept it.** A buyout is usually at their discretion.
- **If your new employer offers to cover it, get that in the offer letter**, along with whether it's
  reimbursed up front or after you join, and any conditions attached.
- **Ask how it will be taxed.** A reimbursement can show up as taxable income.

## Weighing offers when you have time

A long notice period gives you something most job seekers don't have: time to compare. Use it.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Write every offer down the same way">
    Fixed pay, variable pay, join date, buyout support, role and who you'd
    report to. A spoken offer isn't an offer.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Don't accept to keep a backup">
    Accepting an offer you don't intend to take, then dropping it near your join
    date, burns a bridge with a company that may hire you later, and with the
    recruiter who'll remember.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Decide before you negotiate with your current employer">
    If you'd stay for a counter-offer, say so before you resign. A counter-offer
    can fix the pay and leave every other reason you wanted to go untouched.
  </Step>
</Steps>
