Say you're hiring a backend lead in Hyderabad. Your favourite candidate signs the offer on 3
September and starts serving a 90-day notice. You send a welcome email, tell the team, and close
the role. On 28 November, four days before the joining date, an email arrives: "After a lot of
thought, I've decided to stay with my current company."

You did everything right up to the signature, and then stopped. In India, a signed offer is the
start of a two or three-month period in which your candidate can still be persuaded by someone else. The
companies that keep their joiners treat that time as part of hiring, not as waiting.

## Why the gap is so dangerous

Most Indian professionals serve 60 or 90 days of <Term id="notice-period">notice</Term>. That's
two or three months in which they keep interviewing, other recruiters keep calling, and their current
employer finds out they're leaving. Three things usually happen in that window.

- **A <Term id="counter-offer">counter-offer</Term>.** Their manager matches your number, often
  with a promise about a promotion.
- **A better offer.** Your offer gives them a price to quote to the next company.
- **Doubt.** They hear nothing from you for eight weeks and start to wonder whether the team
  remembers them.

You can't stop the first two from being made. You can make sure your offer still feels like the
best one when they arrive.

## The experience before the offer counts too

Candidates who had a slow or confusing process arrive at the offer with less goodwill, and spend it
faster.

A process that took six weeks to decide also tells the candidate how the company works. Every week
you take before the offer is a week in which they can collect another one. We've written about [what a slow hire costs](/en/blogs/cost-of-a-slow-hire), and the
calculator there puts a number on each extra day.

## Keep in touch on a schedule

The fix is not a weekly "just checking in" email from the recruiter. It's real contact with the
people they'll work with, at a steady pace, so joining feels like it has already started.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Week one: a call from the hiring manager">
    Not HR. The person they'll report to, saying they're glad and what the first
    project is likely to be.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Each month: one real touchpoint">
    An invitation to a team lunch or demo, a short call with a future colleague,
    or an early look at the codebase or roadmap.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Before the last month: the practical details">
    Laptop, access, first-week plan and joining-day logistics, sent early.
    Paperwork makes the move feel real.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Two weeks out: ask directly">
    "Is anything worrying you about joining?" Most counter-offers are visible by
    then, and you can still respond.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Talk about the counter-offer before it arrives

Ask during the offer conversation: "If your company makes a counter-offer, what would it need to
include for you to stay?" Many candidates will tell you, and the answer tells you whether
you're competing on money, on the role, or on something you can't change.

If they say "nothing would make me stay", note why. When the counter comes, remind them of their own
reasons, in their words. That often works better than raising your number.

## Shorten the window if you can

The shortest notice period is the one you help end early. Offering to cover part of a <Term
id="notice-buyout">notice buyout</Term> costs less than restarting a search that took two months.
Candidates who join in 30 days instead of 90 have two fewer months of offers to weigh. We've
written up [how buyouts work from the candidate's side](/en/blogs/notice-period-job-hunt), which
helps when you suggest one.

## What not to do

Some employers respond to dropouts by threatening candidates: shared blacklists, demands for
deposits, legal letters. They rarely bring anyone back, and candidates talk to each other. The
companies that win in this market are the ones candidates want to join, not the ones that scare
them into joining.

Track your offer-to-join rate instead, by role and by notice length. If one team loses half its
joiners, the problem is in that process.

## For your next hire

Go back to the backend lead who stayed. The signature was on 3 September, and the next real
conversation was the email on 28 November. Put four dates in your calendar the day the next offer is
signed: the manager's call, two monthly touchpoints and the two-week check. Then give the
counter-offer conversation its own slot, before the candidate's manager has the chance to start it.
