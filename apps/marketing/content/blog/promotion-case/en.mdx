Say your manager's review forms are due on Friday. On Wednesday afternoon they message you:
"Anything you want me to include?" You have two days to remember a year of work, and most of what
comes back is from the last six weeks.

A promotion case built that way rarely wins. The ones that do are built through the year. They're
a running record of what you shipped and what it changed, matched to what the next level expects
and handed to your manager in words they can repeat.

## Your manager argues the case in a room you're not in

In most companies, promotions are decided in a calibration meeting. Managers put their people
forward, compare them against the same level, and a handful of senior people who've never seen
your work make the call.

That changes what your job is. You're not trying to convince your manager, who probably agrees
already. You're giving them the evidence to convince a room of people who don't know you. "Really
strong this year" loses to "cut the release cycle from two weeks to three days and trained two new
joiners to run it".

Waiting to be noticed feels modest. In practice it hands the argument to someone with less
information than you, on a deadline.

## Keep a running record

The fix for Wednesday's message is a note you add to every Friday. Five minutes, three lines:

- **What shipped**, or what you moved forward, this week.
- **What it changed**, with a number when there is one: hours saved, errors down, a customer kept.
- **Who saw it**, so there's someone outside your team who can say so.

Most weeks the entries are small. By the end of a quarter they add up to a list most people can't
produce on demand, and by review time you're choosing your best examples instead of searching for
any. The same habit helps if you're [aiming for a lead role](/en/blogs/ic-to-lead), where the work
is harder to point at.

## Agree what the next level looks like

Every company has some description of what each level expects, even if it's a paragraph in a wiki
nobody reads. Find it, and ask your manager how it's applied in practice. The written version and
the one the calibration room uses are often different.

Then sit down together and go through it line by line:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Mark what you already do">
    Put an example from your record next to each expectation you're already
    meeting. These are the strongest parts of the case.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Name the gaps">
    Agree on the one or two expectations you're not meeting yet. Be specific:
    "leads a project across two teams", not "more leadership".
  </Step>
  <Step title="Pick the work that closes them">
    Ask for the project, the presentation or the on-call rotation that would let
    you show it before the next cycle.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Write down what you agreed and send it to your manager the same day. A short note turns a
conversation into a plan both of you can check against later.

## Have the conversation months before the cycle

The worst time to ask for a promotion is the week the forms are due. By then the list of names is
mostly decided, and there's no time to close a gap.

<Compare
  avoid="I think I deserve a promotion this year. I've been here three years and I work really hard."
  prefer="I'd like to be considered for senior in the next cycle. Here's where I think I'm already meeting the level, and here's the gap. Can we agree on what you'd need to see by March?"
/>

The second version gives your manager something to do. It also makes the outcome less of a
surprise either way, which is better for both of you.

## If the answer is no

Sometimes the case doesn't get through. Ask what tipped it, and get the answer in specifics: which
expectation, which example wasn't strong enough, who in the room raised it. Then agree on a date to
check progress, well before the next cycle.

If the answers keep moving, or the gap is something the role will never let you show, that's
useful to know too. The record you've kept is the same evidence another employer will want to hear
about, and [talking about your work](/en/blogs/talk-about-your-work) in an interview starts from
exactly that kind of list.

## Start the note this Friday

Open a note this Friday and write three lines about this week. Do it again next week. When the
Wednesday message comes, "Anything you want me to include?", you'll answer it in ten minutes with
the best examples from a whole year, already matched to the level you're aiming for.
