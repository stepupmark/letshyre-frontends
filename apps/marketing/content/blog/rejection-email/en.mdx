Imagine you're the recruiter for a product designer role in Mumbai. You get 212 applications, run
18 screening calls, review five portfolios, and ask two finalists for a case study and a 90-minute
panel. On Friday you make an offer, and it's accepted.

That's one happy candidate and 211 waiting to hear from you. The one who waits longest is usually
the runner-up, who gave you a weekend of work. Rejections are most of what a hiring process
produces, and candidates remember them long after they've forgotten your interview questions. A
rejection that's fast, clear and kind costs you ten minutes and keeps the door open.

## Reply at every stage, on a deadline

Silence is the worst rejection, because the candidate keeps waiting and reads it as contempt. Set a
deadline for each stage and keep to it.

| Stage                  | Reply within            | How                           |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Application            | A week after you decide | Email, sent in bulk is fine   |
| Screening call         | Two working days        | Personal email                |
| Interview rounds       | Two working days        | Personal email, with a reason |
| Final round, take-home | Two working days        | A short call, then an email   |

Track it in your <Term id="applicant-tracking-system">applicant tracking system</Term> the way you
track time to hire. A role isn't closed until everyone in it has heard.

## Say no in the first line

Long rejections hide the answer in the third paragraph, after a lot of praise the reader can't
trust. Put the decision first, then the thanks.

<Compare
  avoid="Thank you for your interest. After careful consideration of many strong profiles, we have decided to move forward with candidates whose experience more closely matches our current requirements."
  prefer="Thank you for the time you put into the case study. We've decided not to move forward with your application for the product designer role."
/>

The second version is shorter and kinder. It names what they did, gives the decision once, and
doesn't make them decode it.

## Give a reason when they've earned one

Someone who applied and didn't get a call doesn't need feedback. Someone who did a take-home and a
panel does, and they'll value one honest sentence more than three paragraphs.

Take the reason from the panel's notes, and keep it about the job. If you run a

<Term id="structured-interview">structured interview</Term>, the scorecard
already has it: "The panel wanted more depth on research. Your case study went
straight to screens without showing how you'd test the problem." Don't compare
them to the person you hired, and never mention anything outside the job, such
as age, family or where they live.

Some legal teams prefer no feedback at all. A job-related reason that matches the written
scorecard is the safer middle ground, and it's what candidates are asking for.

## Promise only what you'll do

"We'll keep your profile on file" is the most common line in rejection emails and usually not true.
Write it only if someone will search that file. The same goes for "we'd love you to apply again".

For your runner-up, mean it. Tell them they were close, ask if you can contact them about the next
role, and put a reminder in your calendar. A finalist who already passed your panel is the
cheapest hire you'll ever make, and most teams lose them by sending the same email as everyone else.

## Automate the routine ones, not the late ones

Bulk rejections from the ATS are fine for applications you didn't take forward, if they're well
written and sent promptly. The later the stage, the more personal the message should be. After a
final round, the hiring manager or recruiter calls first, briefly, and the email follows.

## Before your next role closes

Go back to the designer role. 211 emails sounds like a lot, but most were one bulk send. The 18
after screening calls took an hour. Two calls on Friday took 20 minutes. Put those three tasks in
the calendar on the day you open the next role, and close it only when every candidate has heard.
