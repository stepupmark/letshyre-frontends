Paste your resume into any AI tool and ask it to "make this more impressive". You'll get back
something smooth, confident and full of words like _spearheaded_, _leveraged_ and _results-driven_.

Now imagine a recruiter reading forty of those before lunch.

That's the problem with resumes in 2026. The tools didn't make resumes worse. They made them the
same. And a document that sounds like everyone else's gets read like everyone else's: quickly, and
without much belief.

## Polish stopped being a signal

For a long time, a clean, well-written resume told an employer something. It said you took care,
or could write, or at least knew someone who could. That signal is mostly gone.

Economists at [Tilburg University](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.5185035) saw the effect with real
recruiters. AI-assisted cover letters were better written on average, but they didn't win more
interviews, because the improvement landed in the parts recruiters had already learned to skim.
We've written about [what employers read instead](/en/blogs/resumes-look-alike). This post is the
practical side: what to do with your own resume.

The short version is that a resume now has one job, which is to make someone believe you did
specific things, so they want to ask you about them.

## Cut these first

Most resumes get better by getting shorter. These are the parts that take up space without
changing anyone's mind.

<Figure
  src={keepCut}
  srcDark={keepCutDark}
  alt="Two columns. Cut: a summary paragraph of adjectives, a skills list of thirty keywords, duties copied from the job ad, personal details like date of birth, marital status and a photo, and references available on request. Keep: results with a number and a scale, the decisions you made, accurate titles and dates, links to work people can see, and a one-line note for any career break"
  width={720}
  height={320}
  caption="The left column is what AI tools add most readily. The right column is what they can't make up for you."
/>

- **The summary paragraph.** "A results-driven professional with a passion for excellence" is the
  sentence every tool writes first. If you keep a summary, make it one line that says what you do
  and at what level: "Backend engineer, eight years, payments and high-traffic APIs."
- **The keyword wall.** A skills section with thirty tools in it tells the reader you've heard of
  thirty tools. Keep the ones you'd be comfortable being questioned on.
- **Duties.** "Responsible for stakeholder management" describes the job, not you. Anyone in the role
  could write it.
- **Personal details.** Date of birth, marital status, a photo and your father's name are still
  common on Indian resumes. They don't help you get hired, and they invite the kind of judgement you
  don't want.
- **"References available on request."** Everyone knows. Use the line for something else.

## Keep, and sharpen, these

<Steps>
  <Step title="Results, with the size of the thing">
    Not just "reduced costs by 20%", but 20% of what. A team of four and a
    company of four thousand are different jobs, and the reader needs to know
    which one you did.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The decisions you made">
    What you chose, and sometimes what you chose not to do. Interviewers want to
    hear about this part of your work, so give them the hook.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Accurate titles and dates">
    Boring, and the first thing a background check confirms. If there's a gap,
    give it [one honest line](/en/blogs/explain-a-career-gap).
  </Step>
  <Step title="Links to work people can see">
    A shipped product, a portfolio, a talk, a public repository. Anything a
    reader can open is worth more than a sentence describing it.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## One bullet, rewritten three times

Here's what the difference looks like in practice. The same piece of work, written three ways.

<Figure
  src={rewrite}
  srcDark={rewriteDark}
  alt="One resume bullet in three versions, each better than the last. First, responsible for improving data pipeline performance, which is a duty. Second, improved data pipeline performance by 40%, which has a number but no context. Third, cut the nightly pipeline from six hours to ninety minutes by moving joins into the warehouse, which says what, how and how much"
  width={720}
  height={280}
  caption="The third version is the only one an interviewer can ask a good question about."
/>

The first version is a job description. The second is what most AI tools produce when you ask them
to "add metrics": a number with nothing around it, which invites the question "40% of what?" The
third tells the reader what changed, by how much and what you did to change it. It also tells them
exactly what to ask you in the interview, which is the point.

<Compare
  avoid="Spearheaded cross-functional initiatives to drive operational excellence and enhance customer satisfaction."
  prefer="Rebuilt the refund process with the support and finance teams. Refunds went from ten days to three, and refund complaints halved in a quarter."
/>

## How to use AI without sounding like it

None of this means you shouldn't use AI tools on your resume. They're good at some of the work. The
trick is to give them the right jobs.

- **Use them to find gaps, not to fill them.** "Which of these bullets has no result?" is a great
  prompt. "Write me some results" is how fabricated numbers end up on resumes.
- **Use them to cut.** Ask for a version 30% shorter, then check that nothing true got lost.
- **Use them to tailor, honestly.** Paste the job description and ask which of your real experiences
  match it best. Then reorder, don't invent.
- **Read every line aloud.** If a sentence sounds like something you'd never say in an interview,
  rewrite it in your own words. You'll have to say it in the interview anyway.

<Callout tone="warning">
  Never let a tool add a number, tool, title or achievement you can't explain.
  Interviewers will ask about the most impressive line on your resume first, and
  "the AI wrote that" is not an answer.
</Callout>

## What about the applicant tracking system?

You'll often read that most resumes are rejected by software before a human ever sees them. That
claim has been repeated for years without a published study behind it, and it leads people to stuff
their resumes with keywords, which makes them worse to read.

What's true is simpler. Many companies use software to store and search applications, and a resume
in a plain layout is easier for that software and for people. Use a standard file format, normal
headings like "Experience" and "Education", and no text inside images or tables. Then write for the
human who'll read it.

## Your resume is the trailer, not the film

A resume used to carry most of the weight in getting an interview. Now it mostly decides which
questions you get asked. Write it so those questions are about your best work.

Before you send the next one, read each bullet and ask what question it would make a recruiter ask.
Where the answer is "none", cut it, or rewrite it around a number or a decision. The recruiter with
forty resumes before lunch stops on the bullets that make them curious.
