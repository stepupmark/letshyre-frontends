Read twenty applications for the same role this year and something odd happens around number
eight. The sentences start to rhyme. Everyone is "passionate about building scalable solutions".
Everyone "thrives in fast-paced, cross-functional environments". The cover letters are fluent,
well structured and completely interchangeable.

None of these people are lying. They're using the same tools to write, and the tools have a house
style. The result is a strange new problem in hiring: the applications got better, and they
got less useful.

## Polish used to be a signal

Economists have a word for what a good application used to do. It was a _signal_: something costly
to produce, so producing it told you something. A sharp, specific cover letter took an evening and
some self-knowledge. People who bothered tended to care, and people who could write it well could
often think clearly too. Recruiters weren't reading the words so much as the effort behind them.

Generative AI removed the cost. A polished letter now takes thirty seconds, so polish stops telling
you anything.

This isn't a hunch. In a 2024 survey of 3,290 job candidates, Gartner found that close to four in
ten had used AI while applying, most often to write their resume and cover letter.

Hiring teams have noticed. In [Willo's 2026 hiring trends report](https://www.onrec.com/news/news-archive/hiring-trends-report-2026-study-finds-ai-pushing-employers-away-from-traditional), 77% of the teams surveyed said they
regularly see AI-generated or AI-assisted applications. It's a small survey, just over a hundred
hiring professionals, but it matches what anyone who has screened for a role this year will tell
you.

## Recruiters can't reliably tell, and it doesn't help anyway

The obvious response is to spot the AI letters and discount them. A [team of economists at Tilburg
University](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.5185035) tested whether that works, with real recruiters from companies including Philips and PwC
judging real cover letters.

What they found is more interesting than "recruiters can't tell". The AI-assisted letters scored
better on average, especially for weaker applicants. But the better letters didn't win more
interview invitations, because the improvement landed in the formulaic parts of the letter, the
parts recruiters had already learned to skim. When recruiters were told which letters used AI, they
rewarded the strong letters written without it.

<Callout>
  The study's conclusion is the uncomfortable part. When everyone can produce a
  good letter, the letter carries less information, and matching people to jobs
  gets harder for everyone. Better writing made the market blurrier.
</Callout>

So spotting the AI isn't really the point. Even a letter you're sure a human wrote doesn't tell you
much anymore, if the human could have had it written for them.

## Employers are reading something harder to fake

What's happening instead is a quiet shift in what counts as evidence. If a document can be generated,
employers trust it less, and trust what a person has to do live more.

The Willo report puts numbers on that shift. Live behavioural interviews, where a candidate talks
through real examples from their own work, were the most trusted indicator of talent, picked by 68%
of respondents. Four in ten said they were actively moving away from CV-first hiring, and almost
half had changed their interviews to probe deeper.

The step from a live interview to a recorded one matters more than it looks. A live call is gone
when it ends, and what's left is one interviewer's notes and memory. A recording can be watched again,
by someone else, next to another candidate answering the same question. That's the difference
between an impression and evidence.

## Then people started faking the interview

Every new check gets tested. Once interviews became the thing that mattered, some candidates started
gaming those too: reading AI-written answers off a second screen, or sending someone else to the
call.

Gartner's surveys suggest it's still rare, but not negligible. In a mid-2025 survey of about 3,000
candidates, 6% admitted to some form of interview fraud, either posing as someone else or having
someone pose as them. Gartner's analysts went further and
[predicted](https://www.personneltoday.com/hr/by-2028-one-in-four-candidate-profiles-will-be-fake) that by 2028, one in four
candidate profiles worldwide will be fake. That's a forecast, not a measurement, and it's worth
treating as one. It's also a sign of where the effort is heading.

There's a twist on the other side of the table, too. The same Gartner research found that candidates
don't trust the AI judging them any more than employers trust the AI writing to them.

That gap is the real hiring problem of 2026. Neither side can take the other's paperwork at face
value anymore.

## If you're hiring

You don't need new software to adjust to this. You need to stop asking documents to do a job they
can't do anymore.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Read resumes for facts, not voice">
    Dates, employers, scope, the size of what someone ran. Ignore adjectives.
    "Passionate" and "results-driven" are now free.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Ask about one real piece of work">
    Have the candidate walk through something specific they built or fixed, then
    ask what they'd do differently. Generated answers run out of detail on the
    second follow-up question.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Ask everyone the same questions">
    Comparing candidates only works when they answered the same thing. Write the
    questions once per role and score answers before anyone discusses them.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Keep a record you can go back to">
    Notes at the very least, and a recording where the candidate agrees. Memory
    blurs after the fifth interview of the week.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Let a person decide">
    Use AI to save time on volume. In the Willo survey, 79% of hiring teams said
    the final decision has to stay human-led, and not one respondent wanted
    automation to run every stage.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## If you're applying

The advice is almost the reverse. If your application reads like everyone else's, it gets read like
everyone else's.

- **Use AI for the chores, not the substance.** Formatting, tightening, catching typos: fine. But
  the examples, the numbers and the decisions have to be yours, because those are what an
  interviewer will ask about.
- **Put specifics where the adjectives were.** "Cut the release cycle from two weeks to three days"
  says more than any amount of "results-driven", and no tool can make it up for you. We've written
  about [rewriting your resume this way](/en/blogs/resume-after-ai).
- **Prepare to talk, not to recite.** Pick three pieces of work you know inside out. Practise
  explaining what was hard, what you chose and why. That's the part of hiring that's getting more
  weight, and it's the part where experience shows.

## Where this is heading

The resume will stay, as what it probably should have been all along: a table of contents, not the
book. The part that decides who gets hired is moving towards things a person has to do, in a way
someone else can check. That's the bet behind LetsHyre's recorded interviews, and the [first post on
this blog](/en/blogs/why-we-built-letshyre) explains it.

The next time the sentences start to rhyme around application eight, take it as the cue to stop
reading and start asking.
