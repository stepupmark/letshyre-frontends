Say you're Ishita, a product designer in Pune. One evening you find two openings at the same
European company. The Milan role lists a salary range in the advert. The Pune role says
"competitive", and the recruiter's first question on the call is your current CTC.

Same company, same job title, two sets of rules. Under EU rules due by June 2026, an employer must tell
applicants the pay, or a range, before the interview, and can't ask what they earn now. Italy, where
the Milan role is, has already written that into law. India has
no such rule, but the habit is spreading here anyway, and the companies that bring it in first will
set the norm for everyone else.

## What the EU now requires

The [Pay Transparency
Directive](https://riskandcompliance.freshfields.com/post/102me9f/pay-transparency-revolution-applicants-rights-under-the-eu-pay-transparency-dir)
gives job applicants rights that most Indian candidates have never had.

- **The pay comes first.** The starting pay or a range has to be in the advert or shared before the
  interview.
- **No questions about your current pay.** Employers can't ask what you earn now or earned before.
- **Employees can ask.** Once hired, you can ask for your own pay level and the average pay of people
  doing the same work, split by gender.
- **Pay secrecy clauses go.** A contract can't stop you telling colleagues what you earn.
- **Larger employers report pay gaps,** starting with the biggest companies.

## Most countries were late

The deadline for countries to write the rules into their own law was 7 June 2026. Only a few made
it. Italy, Slovakia and Malta [passed full laws on
time](https://www.littler.com/news-analysis/asap/did-member-states-meet-deadline-status-implementation-eu-pay-transparency).
Germany is reportedly aiming for 2027, and Sweden has asked to renegotiate, according to [Lockton's
September
tracker](https://global.lockton.com/us/en/news-insights/eu-pay-transparency-directive-implementation-status).

So in late 2026 the rules apply fully in some EU countries and are on their way in the rest. Large
companies hiring across Europe are mostly setting one policy for the whole region instead of
waiting country by country.

## India is halfway there without a law

There's no Indian rule on pay in job adverts. Employers have moved anyway, at least for some roles.

The range in the advert matters less than the question on the first call. Indian
hiring still runs on "what's your current CTC?", and offers are often worked out as a percentage on
top of it. That's the exact question the EU now bans. It carries a low salary from one job into the
next, and it hits people who were underpaid, took a career break or are switching fields hardest.

Global capability centres sit in the middle. Many belong to EU or US companies that already publish
ranges at home. Expect some of them to bring the same practice to their Indian hiring, starting with
the companies whose head offices are in countries that have passed the law.

## If you're looking for a job

You don't need a law to ask for the band. It's a normal question now, and asking early saves both
sides weeks.

<Compare
  avoid="Could you tell me roughly what this pays? Sorry to ask so early."
  prefer="Before we go further, could you share the band for this role? I want to make sure we're in the same range."
/>

When you're asked for your current CTC, you can answer with what you expect instead. We go through
how in [answering the salary expectations question](/en/blogs/salary-expectations). If the employer
publishes ranges for its European roles, it's fair to say so politely.

## If you're hiring

Publishing a range is mostly a decision you've already made. You have a budget for the role. Putting
it in the advert means candidates who want twice that don't apply, and the ones who do apply already
know the number when the offer arrives.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Write down the band for each role">
    If two people doing the same job are paid very differently, you'll want to
    know why before a candidate asks.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Publish a range that's honest">
    A band from 10 to 40 lakh tells a candidate nothing. Keep it to the range
    you'd really offer.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Stop asking for current CTC">
    Ask what the candidate expects instead, and price the role, not their last
    employer's pay.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## What to watch next

Over 2027 the remaining EU countries will pass their laws, and the first large companies will
publish pay-gap reports. In India, watch the global companies first. If GCCs start listing
ranges, candidates will start expecting them from everyone hiring the same people.

For Ishita, none of that has to wait. On her Pune call she can ask for the band before she gives a
number. The company already wrote one down for Milan.
