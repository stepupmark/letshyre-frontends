Over the past few years, it became fashionable to announce that your company no longer required a
degree. Big names did it. Press releases went out. "Skills-based hiring" moved from HR conference
slides into job ads, and it has stayed there ever since.

Then a team at Harvard Business School and the Burning Glass Institute did something nobody had
bothered to do. They checked who these companies actually hired afterwards.

## The change that mostly didn't happen

The researchers followed large employers that had removed degree requirements from job postings and
looked at their hiring before and after. For most of them, the people being hired barely changed.
The new job ads said "or equivalent experience". The shortlists still said "graduate".

About 45% of the firms made the change in name only. A smaller group, 37% of those that dropped the
requirement, drove nearly all of the real shift, raising their share of hires without a degree by
nearly 20% in the roles studied. Across everyone, Joseph Fuller, one of the authors, put the result
bluntly: the promised change [showed up in not even 1 in 700 hires](https://pw.hks.harvard.edu/post/skills-based-hiring-the-long-road-from-pronouncements-to-practice)
the year before.

That's not a story about bad faith. It's a story about what's easy and what isn't.

## Deleting a line is easy. Checking a skill isn't.

A degree requirement does real work for a busy recruiter. It's a cheap filter. It cuts three hundred
applications to sixty without anyone having to think, and if a hire goes wrong, nobody gets blamed
for picking a graduate.

Take the requirement out of the ad and the filter doesn't disappear. It moves into the recruiter's
head. Faced with the same sixty minutes and the same three hundred applications, people fall back
on the proxies they trust: the college, the last employer, the job title. The report's authors make
the same point. Employers drift back to proxies rather than doing the hard work of assessing what
an individual can actually do.

<Callout>
  Removing a degree requirement doesn't create a skills-based process. It just
  removes the old filter. Unless something replaces it, the old filter comes
  back unofficially.
</Callout>

## Where the firms that did it are winning

The companies that actually changed who they hired weren't doing it for charity. The same report
found that at those firms, people hired without degrees stayed longer: their retention rate was 10
percentage points higher than their degree-holding colleagues in the same roles.

There's also the arithmetic of who you can reach. When LinkedIn's economists modelled hiring on
skills instead of job history and qualifications, the pool of people who qualified for a typical role
grew sharply, and grew most for the people credential filters leave out.

A bigger pool sounds like more work. It is, unless you have a way to check skills that's faster than
reading resumes. That's the whole problem in one sentence.

## What a skill check actually looks like

Most skills on a resume are claims. "Advanced SQL." "Led a team of eight." "Strong communicator."
None of them are lies, necessarily. They're just untested. A skills-based process is one where every
claim that matters for the job gets tested the same way for everyone.

Employers are starting to move this way. In [Willo's 2026 hiring trends report](https://www.onrec.com/news/news-archive/hiring-trends-report-2026-study-finds-ai-pushing-employers-away-from-traditional),
only 37% of hiring teams rated credentials and learning history as a top indicator of talent, and
42% had brought in skills-based assessments. It's a small survey, but the direction is clear.

## Making it real, in five steps

<Steps>
  <Step title="Write down the skills before you write the ad">
    Three to five, phrased as things you could watch someone do. "Breaks a vague
    problem into steps" is a skill. "Self-starter" isn't.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Pick one check per skill">
    A short work sample, a structured interview question about a real past
    example, or a conversation where they explain their work. Keep each one
    under twenty minutes.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Run the checks before the shortlist, not after">
    If the skill check comes after a resume screen, the resume screen still
    decides. Move evidence earlier.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Score against the same scale">
    Agree what a weak, solid and strong answer looks like, and score before
    anyone compares notes.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Look at who you hired, a year later">
    The Burning Glass study worked because it checked outcomes, not intentions.
    Do the same. If the shortlist still looks like last year's, the filter is
    back.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## The honest version of skills-based hiring

Skills-based hiring isn't a policy. You can't announce it. It's a set of habits that make a
candidate's ability visible before their background gets a vote, and it's measured by who ends up
in the job.
