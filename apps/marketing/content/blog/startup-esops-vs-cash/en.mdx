Say you're Neha, a backend engineer in Bengaluru with two offers on the table. A global capability
centre offers ₹30 lakh, all cash. A Series C fintech offers ₹26 lakh plus 8,000 stock options at a
strike price of ₹10. The recruiter adds that the last funding round priced the shares at ₹500, "so
that's ₹39 lakh of ESOPs".

That number is the price of a share someone else bought, times options Neha doesn't own yet. Her
options might be worth nothing, or several times the ₹4 lakh a year she'd give up. The way to choose
is to work out the share price at which the options beat the cash, and then decide whether the
company is likely to get there and let her sell.

## Why "₹39 lakh" isn't ₹39 lakh

Four things sit between the paper value and money in Neha's account.

- **Vesting.** Options usually vest over four years, with nothing in the first year (the cliff),
  then a quarter a year. Leave at month 11 and you leave with none.
- **Exercise costs money.** To own the shares you pay the strike price. For Neha that's small, ₹10
  a share.
- **Tax comes at exercise, not at sale.** The gap between the strike and the share's fair value
  when you exercise is taxed as salary. Exercise 2,000 options at a ₹500 fair value and about ₹9.8
  lakh is added to your income, which is roughly ₹3 lakh of tax, for shares you may not be able to
  sell.
- **You can only sell when someone buys.** Private shares have no market. You get cash through a
  buyback or an IPO, on the company's timeline.

A small number of startups with a specific government certificate can let employees [defer that
tax](https://ksandk.com/labour-employment/esop-taxation-in-india/) until they sell or leave. Most
can't, so ask rather than assume.

## Work out the break-even price

The question is whether the options make up the cash gap. Neha's gap is ₹4 lakh a year, ₹16 lakh
over four years. Her 8,000 options are worth `8,000 × (share price − ₹10)` at the moment she can
sell.

| Share price when she can sell   | Options worth, before tax | Against the ₹16 lakh gap |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Company shuts down              | ₹0                        | ₹16 lakh behind          |
| ₹210, under half the last round | ₹16 lakh                  | Even                     |
| ₹500, the last round's price    | ₹39.2 lakh                | ₹23 lakh ahead           |
| ₹1,000, an IPO at double        | ₹79.2 lakh                | ₹63 lakh ahead           |

Both the salary and the gain on exercise are taxed as salary, so the comparison holds roughly the
same after tax. That changes the question. The fintech doesn't need to grow at all for Neha to come out ahead.
It needs to survive, keep at least ₹210 a share, and give her a way to sell within a few years.

That last condition is where most ESOPs fail. A company can be worth more every year and still
never buy back a share.

## Buybacks are real now, but partial

Liquidity has improved. Indian startups bought back about [$220 million of employee
options](https://entrackr.com/report/indian-startup-esop-buybacks-in-q1-2026-beat-2024-and-2025-2-bn-liquidity-since-2020-11723960)
in the first quarter of 2026, more than in all of 2024 or 2025. But most programmes let you sell a
slice, not the lot. One 2026 buyback capped sales at 5% of vested options, another at 10%, another
at 25%.

So a buyback is a reason to stay calm, not a plan. Ask how often the company has run one, what share
of vested options it covered, and whether former employees could sell too.

## Seven questions to ask before you sign

Recruiters can answer most of these, and the company's ESOP policy document answers the rest. Ask
for it.

<Checklist title="ESOP questions for the recruiter">
  <Check>
    How many shares exist in total, fully diluted, so I can see my percentage?
  </Check>
  <Check>What are the strike price and the latest fair market value?</Check>
  <Check>What is the vesting schedule, and is there a one-year cliff?</Check>
  <Check>How long do I have to exercise vested options after I leave?</Check>
  <Check>Has the company run buybacks, and could former employees sell?</Check>
  <Check>Is the company eligible to defer the tax on exercise?</Check>
  <Check>What happens to unvested options if the company is acquired?</Check>
</Checklist>

The exit window matters more than it looks. Many policies give you 90 days after you leave to
exercise and pay the tax, or lose the options. A longer window, or one that runs until an exit, is
one of the cheapest things a startup can give you.

## Negotiate the mix, not just the total

Once you know the break-even price, you can ask for the trade you prefer. If you have an EMI and two
dependants, more cash is a fair ask, and a startup that can't afford it is telling you something.
If you can live on the lower salary for four years, more options or a longer exit window may be
worth more.

The second ask costs the company almost nothing today, which makes it easier to agree to.

## Before you choose

Write down the share price where the options make up your cash gap. Neha's is ₹210. Then ask
yourself one plain question: would you bet ₹16 lakh that this company will be worth that much and let
you sell within four or five years? If yes, take the options. If you hesitate, the cash offer is the
better job for you, and that's a fine answer.
