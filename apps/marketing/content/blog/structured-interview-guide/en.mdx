Most interviews are conversations that drift. Two interviewers meet the same candidate, ask
different questions, and come back with opposite opinions and no way to settle them. A structured
interview fixes that with a simple rule: every candidate for a role gets the same questions, and
every answer is scored against the same scale, written down before anyone compares notes.

It feels stiff the first time. It is also one of the most reliable ways to predict how someone
will do in the job, and the easiest way to make hiring decisions you can explain afterwards.

## Start from the job, not the candidate

Before you write a single question, decide what the person in this role has to be good at. Not
"a great culture fit" or "strong communicator", but behaviour you could watch: _breaks a vague
problem into steps_, _explains a trade-off to someone outside the team_, _owns a mistake and fixes
it_.

Pick three to five of these criteria. More than five and interviewers stop scoring carefully;
fewer than three and one strong answer decides the whole interview.

<Callout tone="tip">
  Ask the hiring manager to describe the best and the worst person they have
  seen in this role. The difference between the two is usually your list of
  criteria.
</Callout>

## Write questions that produce evidence

Each criterion gets one or two questions, written in advance and asked word for word. Two kinds
work well:

- **Past behaviour:** "Tell me about a time you had to deliver something with an unclear brief.
  What did you do first?" Past behaviour is the best guide to future behaviour, as long as you ask
  for a specific example rather than a general habit.
- **Job samples:** "Here is a short brief we received last month. Walk me through how you would
  approach it." This shows how the candidate thinks, not how well they tell stories.

Avoid hypotheticals that invite the textbook answer ("How would you handle conflict?") and
questions about anything that isn't in the job.

Plan one or two follow-up prompts per question, such as "What was your part in that?" or "What
would you do differently?". Every interviewer uses the same ones, so a candidate isn't rewarded
for meeting a chattier interviewer.

## Agree the scale before the interview

A scorecard turns answers into something you can compare. Use a short scale, one to four works
well, and write down what each point looks like for every criterion before the first interview.

| Rating | What it means                                                    |
| ------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1      | No relevant example, or the example shows the opposite behaviour |
| 2      | A relevant example, but the candidate's own part is unclear      |
| 3      | A clear example where the candidate did what the role needs      |
| 4      | A clear example, repeated or applied in a harder situation       |

An even-numbered scale removes the safe middle score. Interviewers have to decide whether an
answer leans towards the bar or away from it.

## Run the interview the same way every time

Open with two minutes on how the interview works: how many questions, that you will take notes,
and that there will be time for their questions at the end. Candidates do better when they know
the shape of the conversation, and you get a truer picture of them.

Then ask the questions in order. Take notes on what the candidate actually says, not on your
impression of it. "Rebuilt the release checklist after the outage and ran it for six months" is
evidence; "seems very organised" is not.

<Callout>
  Score each answer straight after the interview, on your own. Ratings given
  after hearing a colleague's opinion measure the colleague, not the candidate.
</Callout>

## Debrief on evidence

In the debrief, go criterion by criterion. Each interviewer reads out their rating and the
evidence behind it before anyone argues for a decision. Where ratings differ by two points or
more, look at the notes: often one interviewer simply didn't ask the follow-up.

The decision then follows from the scores and the bar you set at the start, rather than from
whoever speaks with the most confidence in the room.

## A short checklist

1. Three to five criteria, written as behaviour you could observe.
2. The same questions and follow-ups for every candidate.
3. A one-to-four scale, described in advance for each criterion.
4. Notes on what was said, scored alone and straight away.
5. A debrief that compares evidence before opinions.
