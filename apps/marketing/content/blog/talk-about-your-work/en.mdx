Most experienced people are worse at interviews than their work deserves. Not because they're
nervous, but because they describe their careers the way a resume does: in summaries.

"I led the migration to the new platform." "I improved performance significantly." "I worked closely
with stakeholders." All true. All forgettable. And to an interviewer who's heard the same sentences
from three other candidates that week, impossible to tell apart from someone who watched the
migration from the next team.

## What interviewers are listening for

A good interviewer isn't scoring your vocabulary. They're trying to answer one question: did this
person really do the thing, and do they understand why it worked?

That's why behavioural questions, the "tell me about a time when" kind, have become the most trusted
part of hiring. In [Willo's 2026 hiring trends survey](https://www.onrec.com/news/news-archive/hiring-trends-report-2026-study-finds-ai-pushing-employers-away-from-traditional),
68% of hiring teams named live behavioural interviews with real examples as their most trusted
indicator of talent, ahead of resumes and credentials. The research backs them up.

So when an interviewer asks about your work, you're in the part of the process that matters most.
The good news is that it's also the part you can prepare for best.

## One answer, five parts

A believable answer has a shape. You've probably heard of STAR: situation, task, action, result.
It's a fine start, but it leaves out the two things experienced interviewers listen for most: the
decision you made, and what you learned.

<Steps>
  <Step title="The problem">
    One or two sentences. What was going wrong, and why it mattered. Skip the
    company history.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Your part">
    What you personally owned. Say "I" when it was you and "we" when it was the
    team. Interviewers notice when every sentence says "we".
  </Step>
  <Step title="The decision">
    The choice you made, and the alternative you didn't take. This is where you
    show judgement, not effort.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The result">
    What changed, with a number if there is one. "Within a day", "from two weeks
    to three days", "the team stopped getting paged at night".
  </Step>
  <Step title="What you'd change">
    One honest thing you'd do differently. It's the strongest signal that you
    understood what happened, rather than just being there.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## The test is the second follow-up

Here's what most interview advice misses. Anyone can prepare a good first answer. The interviewer
knows that. What they're really waiting for is the follow-up, and then the one after that.

This is also why a memorised, AI-polished answer doesn't work for long. It sounds good once. Ask
"why that and not the alternative?" and there's nothing underneath it, because the person saying it
never had to weigh the alternative.

You don't beat follow-ups by preparing more lines. You beat them by picking stories you know inside
out, and remembering the details that a summary throws away: who disagreed, what nearly went wrong,
what you'd tried first.

## Build your five stories this week

<Callout tone="tip">
  Do this once and you'll use it for every interview in your search. Most
  behavioural questions are variations on a handful of themes.
</Callout>

- **Something you built or shipped** that you're proud of, and why it was hard.
- **Something that went wrong** and what you did about it. This is the one people dodge, and one of the
  most telling when it's answered well.
- **A disagreement** with a manager, peer or client, and how it got resolved.
- **A decision you made with incomplete information**, and how it turned out.
- **Something you changed about how a team works**, whether that's a process, a tool or a habit.

For each one, write the five parts in a sentence or two each. Then say it out loud, to a friend or to
your phone. Answers that look fine on paper often run three minutes when spoken. Aim for about
ninety seconds, and let the interviewer pull out the rest.

## A few things that quietly undo good answers

- **Numbers you can't explain.** If you say "40% faster", expect "faster at what, measured how?" Use
  numbers you can defend, or describe the change in plain words.
- **Hiding the team.** Claiming every success as yours makes interviewers doubt all of it. Be exact
  about what was yours.
- **Skipping the failure.** "I can't think of a mistake" is heard as "I don't notice my mistakes".
- **Talking about tools instead of decisions.** What you used matters less than why you chose it.

## Practise where it counts

The best practice is the real thing, out loud, with questions you didn't write yourself. Ask a
friend to pick three questions for the role you want and to push back twice on each answer, the way
a good interviewer would. Record it on your phone and listen once. You'll hear every "I led the
migration" that still needs its story.
