Say you're Joel, a customer support lead in Kochi with five years on a SaaS help desk. You upload
your resume to LetsHyre, and the AI suggests three roles: Customer Support Lead, QA Analyst and
Technical Account Manager. You want out of support, you have three interviews, and the obvious move
is one interview for each.

That's often the wrong move. Every attempt counts, whichever role it's for. Employers match you on
the role you interviewed for, and they unlock each interview separately. Three thin attempts at
three different jobs can leave you with no strong scorecard for the one you want. Start with the
role you most want to be hired for, and decide the other two after you've seen the first result.

## How the three interviews work

A few rules shape every decision here, so it's worth having them straight.

- **You get up to three interviews in total**, and every attempt counts, whichever role it's for.
- **Each interview is for one role**, which you choose just before you start. The role from your
  onboarding is already selected, and you can switch it.
- **Each produces its own scorecard.** Employers you apply to see your latest one.
- **Employers unlock interviews one at a time.** When an employer looks at you, the interview
  closest to their job's role is marked, so they start there.

That last point matters most. An employer hiring a QA analyst cares about your QA interview. A great
support interview won't stand in for one.

## Start with the role you want most

Your first interview should be for the job you'd take tomorrow, not the one you'd be safest in.
For Joel that's QA Analyst. It's the move he wants, and a strong QA scorecard is the evidence a
hiring manager needs to take a chance on a support lead.

If you're not sure which role that is, look at the jobs open now under Find jobs. The role with
more openings you'd want is the better first bet.

## Use the second interview for a retake or a neighbour

Once you've seen your first scorecard, you have real information. There are two good uses for the
second interview, and the scorecard tells you which.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Retake the same role">
    If you scored lower than you expected and can see why, prepare for that gap
    and retake it. A weak spoken answer or a coding question you ran out of time
    on are both fixable. Retake only once you've changed something, not straight
    after.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Try a neighbouring role">
    If the scorecard was solid, use the second interview on a role close to it,
    where your background still fits. For Joel, QA Analyst first, then Technical
    Account Manager, which leans on his customer experience.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Remember that employers you apply to see your latest scorecard. A retake that goes worse is the one
they'll see, which is one more reason to prepare before you take it.

## Keep the third in reserve

You don't need to use all three at once. A third interview held back is useful later, when your
skills have moved on, or when a role you want starts appearing in the job list.

Joel keeps his for the role he's least sure of. If QA works out, he may never need it.

## Pick roles your resume can back up

Matching uses both your interview and your resume. A strong interview for a role your background
doesn't support will match weakly on role fit, and employers can see which of their job's skills
you have and which you're missing.

When you type a role that wasn't suggested, add a line on why it fits, and make sure your resume
shows it: a project, a certification, the part of your current job that's closest. For career
switchers, our post on [talking about your work](/en/blogs/talk-about-your-work) helps with the
same story in your spoken answers.

## Before your first interview

Write down the one role you'd take tomorrow, the neighbouring role you'd try second, and what would
make you retake instead. Then read [what happens on interview day](/en/blogs/first-ai-interview),
so the attempt you've planned so carefully isn't lost to a browser left open.
