Say you're the engineering manager at a logistics startup in Bengaluru, hiring a backend engineer.
You've unlocked four candidates on LetsHyre, and you have a first call booked with each. Your usual
plan for that call is the one most managers use: "Walk me through your experience", then a couple
of technical questions, then "Any questions for us?"

Half of that plan is already done. The candidate has answered role-based questions on camera, and
the scorecard shows how each answer was assessed. If you watch the parts that matter first, the
first call can start where the interview left off, and you'll learn twice as much from it.

## Start with the summary, not the video

Each unlocked interview opens on its scorecard. Read the summary before you press play.

- **The score out of 100**, and how many answers it's based on.
- **Fit for the job**: the overall match, role fit and skills fit, and why the AI matched them.
- **Communication**, scored for clarity, relevance, confidence, depth of thought and the key
  concepts covered. It's how you tell a correct answer from a convincing one.
- **Strengths, areas to improve and recommendations**, which sum the interview up.

Then read down the answers. Each question is listed with the candidate's answer and the AI's
assessment, grouped by type: multiple choice, pseudocode, coding, written and spoken.

## Pick the answers worth watching

You don't need to watch the whole recording. Use the scorecard to choose where to look, and jump
straight to those questions.

<Steps>
  <Step title="The answer closest to the job">
    For a backend role, the system design or data question. Watch how they
    reason, not just whether they got there.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The weakest-scored answer">
    Was it a gap in knowledge, or a nervous answer to a question they plainly
    understood? The assessment can't always tell; you can.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Anything in areas to improve">
    If the scorecard says "shallow on trade-offs", watch the answer that earned
    it, and decide if you agree.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Write one note per answer you watch: what convinced you, and what you'd ask next.

## Rewrite the first call around what's left

The interview has covered the basics. Your call should go where it couldn't: deeper into their own
work, the decisions behind it, and how they'd handle your team's problems.

<Compare
  avoid="Walk me through your experience. Then: how would you design a rate limiter?"
  prefer="In the interview you chose a token bucket for the rate limiter. On your current team, where did a design like that break down, and what did you change?"
/>

The second question can only be asked by someone who watched. Candidates notice, and it tells them
their interview counted for something.

A useful plan for each call has three parts.

- **One question that builds on their best answer**, to see how far the depth goes.
- **One that revisits their weakest**, to give them a fair second chance.
- **One about your actual work**, a real problem your team has now, which no interview could cover.

## Bring the hiring panel in

If someone else on the panel will meet the candidate, share the scorecard with them before the call
instead of forwarding notes. A shared link shows the scorecard and profile without contact details,
lasts up to 30 days, and can be limited to a number of views. Scorecards also print cleanly, if your
panel prefers paper.

Ask each person to pick their own answer to watch, and compare notes before the call. You'll avoid
three people asking the same question.

## Before your next first call

Back to the backend hire. For each of the four candidates, the manager reads the summary, watches
three answers, and writes a three-question plan. The calls skip the warm-up and go straight to
the work. Two candidates are clearly ahead by the end of the week, and nobody had to ask "tell me
about yourself".

so the interviews you open are the ones worth watching.
